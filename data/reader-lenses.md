# Reader Lens — what readers reward and punish, by genre

_Generated 2026-11-09 from **1,330** reader reviews. Source: UCSD Book Graph (Goodreads) — PROVISIONAL first pass, 190 reviews per genre._

Every line below is **guidance, not a rule**: it describes what readers of a genre reward or punish in their own reviews, so the AI author can deviate deliberately rather than by accident. The number after each line is the evidence count — how many reviews in the sample supported it. Scene kinds are **specific to each genre**, discovered from what its readers actually discuss; the planner tags each section with one or two of them and the writer receives that scene's guidance.

## Erotica & erotic romance
<a id="erotica"></a>

**Key:** `erotica` · **Reviews:** 190 · **Matches genre labels like:** erotica, erotic, erotic romance, bdsm, kink, steamy, explicit romance, adult romance, smut, dark romance

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Readers rate sexual tension separately from sex scenes and usually above them; the slow build before consummation is the prize  _(26)_
- A real plot (mystery, revenge, suspense, business stakes) braided through the romance turns steam into a book readers rave about  _(22)_
- Humor and quick banter alongside the heat are praised again and again; funny plus filthy is the winning combination  _(20)_
- The commanding or dirty-mouthed hero wins when layers peel back to reveal a good man underneath; readers want to understand him  _(17)_
- Readers prize books that gut them: raw vulnerability, heartbreak, tears; emotion evoked is worth stars on its own  _(17)_
- A heroine who drives the story, pushes back, keeps self-respect and matches the hero's wit is singled out for praise  _(13)_
- Dark or predatory heroes work when readers are let inside his head and the heroine keeps her spirit intact under pressure  _(9)_
- Dual POV, especially a convincing male voice, is repeatedly named as the best part even of otherwise weak books  _(7)_

**Readers punish**
- A doormat heroine who obeys, excuses abuse, or gives up all agency is the single most common 1-star complaint  _(18)_
- Alpha that tips into misogyny, stalking, cruelty after sex or unrepentant abuse is called out; 'this is not an alpha, this is an abuser'  _(16)_
- Recognizable templates (billionaire, virgin, instalove, FSoG clone) read as derivative unless something new is on the table  _(15)_
- When sex replaces plot, scenes blur together and readers openly admit skimming or skipping them  _(13)_
- Serial splitting, padded page counts and under-150-page novellas are read as cash grabs that leave characters undeveloped  _(13)_
- Leads with no felt connection beyond lust leave readers 'disconnected'; chemistry cannot be asserted, it must be shown  _(11)_
- Contrived angst that one honest conversation would resolve, repeated in cycles, is called out as filler  _(9)_
- Characters who contradict their premise (abductor turns whiny, Dom turns timid, smart woman acts like a teen) break belief  _(8)_

**They come in expecting**
- Readers grant fantasy license to possessive, over-the-top heroes, but the story must still make internal sense  _(10)_
- A HEA or HFN is assumed; a cliffhanger is tolerated only when the book is clearly flagged as part of a duet or serial  _(10)_
- Readers track 'safety' (other partners, cheating, condoms, virginity, triggers) and feel ambushed by anything unflagged  _(9)_
- Dominance and kink are welcome when the submissive negotiates and keeps a voice; coercion dressed as D/s is rejected  _(9)_
- The hero sleeping with others once the leads have met is close to unforgivable, even when the plot explains it  _(8)_

**On the prose itself**
- Dirty talk is prized when it is specific and confident in the hero's voice; generic porn-script lines get laughed at  _(19)_
- Repeated heat-scene vocabulary ('mine', 'wet', 'whimper', clenching, throbbing) is counted and mocked; vary the language  _(10)_
- Lyrical, quotable lines get highlighted and quoted in reviews; readers notice when a writer is a writer  _(9)_
- Tense slips, missing contractions, unmarked POV switches and typos are named explicitly as reasons for low ratings  _(6)_
- A male POV that sounds like the heroine, or like a 14-year-old, pulls readers out; make his voice distinct  _(4)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- The most common 2-star arc is a strong first third that collapses; an opening full of banter and tension creates a debt  _(8)_
- A sharp premise or meet-cute hooks readers from page one; they name the concept as the reason they kept going  _(7)_
- Sex within the first few pages before character is established earns the 'no plot' verdict for the whole book  _(4)_
- The heroine's first impression (self-pity, body stats, a drunken mess) sticks; readers rarely forgive a bad first glimpse  _(3)_

**Middle**
- A sagging middle where sex substitutes for forward motion makes readers skim, nap or DNF around 40-60%  _(8)_
- The fight-sex-fight loop and recycled jealousy as midbook filler are called out by name  _(5)_
- Heroes who lose their edge midway or in a sequel (less alpha, more moping) disappoint readers who fell for the earlier version  _(5)_
- Push-pull must stay authentic; readers do not want the couple to 'fuck it out' or the hero magically healed by sex  _(3)_

**Ending**
- An epilogue or glimpse years into the future is the most requested and most praised closing move  _(15)_
- A duet ending at a natural turn is accepted; an unflagged cliffhanger or a story chopped to sell parts infuriates  _(10)_
- Reveals from left field and resolutions rushed into the last quarter undercut otherwise liked books  _(6)_
- Without groveling or visible redemption the hero stays unforgiven and the HEA reads as the heroine settling  _(4)_
- A sugary, predictable HEA with a bonus miracle baby produces 'no feels' rather than satisfaction  _(3)_

**dialogue** `dialogue`
- Banter earns its place when each line reveals character or moves the story; characters calling themselves funny is not funny  _(7)_
- Register must fit the world: bikers who speak too properly, priests who curse constantly, or dialect that needs rereading all jar  _(4)_

**action** `action`
- Danger and suspense interwoven with the romance keep non-sex scenes from feeling like filler and raise the heat  _(8)_
- Stacked violence and gore without consequence or aftermath numbs readers rather than thrilling them  _(3)_

**intimacy** `intimacy`
- Anticipation outranks consummation; readers remember the charged scenes before the first time more than the sex itself  _(26)_
- Dirty talk in a distinct, confident voice is a highlight; cheesy or generic lines break the scene into laughter  _(19)_
- Scenes that repeat with no change in stakes or emotion blur together and get skipped; each one must shift something  _(13)_
- Hot scenes carry a weak book to about 3 stars and no further; readers say 'the sex was great' in 2-star reviews  _(12)_
- Sex must carry emotion and connection; choreography that feels mechanical or 'puppeteered' is named as the dealbreaker  _(9)_

- Withholding basic facts (ages, motives, why he is 'bad') frustrates rather than intrigues; readers want them early  _(5)_
- Trauma backstory used as a shortcut to excuse behavior, without depth or research, is called out as lazy  _(4)_

**quiet** `quiet`
- Pages of the heroine's looping inner lust and doubt invite skimming; readers count how often she wants him  _(9)_
- Aftermath must appear on the page; a wound or assault forgotten by the next scene breaks trust in the whole book  _(3)_

## Fantasy, paranormal & speculative
<a id="fantasy-paranormal"></a>

**Key:** `fantasy_paranormal` · **Reviews:** 190 · **Matches genre labels like:** fantasy, paranormal, urban fantasy, epic fantasy, high fantasy, science fiction, sci-fi, speculative, dystopian, horror, …

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Top praise is a cast readers feel bound to; even flawed or annoying characters pass if the reader cares what happens to them  _(30)_
- A world vivid enough to buy without effort, with a magic system readers can grasp, is named again and again as the reason to love  _(22)_
- Being made to ache, cry, or stay up till dawn is the five-star marker; readers reward books that hurt in a beautiful way  _(22)_
- Prose that is beautiful yet plain-spoken wins; readers note when lines are quotable and when the voice goes straight to the feelings  _(17)_
- Snark, banter and dark humor, especially amid grim events, are quoted in review after review as the thing that sold the book  _(15)_
- Twists hinted at so readers can say 'I knew it' or 'ohhh' in hindsight; deliberate misdirection is admired when it plays fair  _(15)_
- Found-family crews, sister-bonds and bromances are praised as 'epic as romance'; camaraderie makes readers feel part of the gang  _(11)_
- Narrators who are cowards, liars, assassins or brutes on paper become favorites when their inner voice is honest and darkly funny  _(9)_

**Readers punish**
- Nothing happening: long stretches where readers skim, force themselves on, or quit at 70% because 'surely it gets better' never pays  _(28)_
- Flat, interchangeable characters the reader can't describe or care about; 'I didn't care' is the death knell even with a cool premise  _(21)_
- Predictability: readers call twists from page one, and a mystery solved by dumb luck or an unforeshadowed twist feels like cheating  _(19)_
- Insta-love: a romance that ignites in a day and halts the real story is the single most repeated gripe, in adult books as well as YA  _(19)_
- Rushed, convenient endings tied in a bow, deus ex machina rescues, or a finale that spends ten pages on the book's stated goal  _(18)_
- Whiny, weepy, impulsive or flawless protagonists; readers lose patience with a lead who cries every chapter or never makes a smart choice  _(18)_
- Vague world or powers never explained; Capitalized Terms, unexplained rules and magic with no clear mechanics read as lazy  _(17)_
- Close imitation of a famous series: readers map the plot beats one-to-one and rate the copy below the original every time  _(11)_

**They come in expecting**
- Women reduced to wives, love objects or stereotypes draw explicit complaint; capable heroines are a stated reason to love a book  _(11)_
- A first book must satisfy on its own; withholding basic answers to sell the sequel is called a cheat, and 'no cliffhanger' is a bonus  _(11)_
- Magic needs rules or a cost; readers praise systems they can follow and resent powers that shift to fit the plot  _(7)_
- In dark fantasy deaths must cost something; a crisis dispensed with no sacrifice feels hollow, while real losses are praised as 'real'  _(6)_
- Retellings are checked against the myth or tale; renaming gods or gutting their nature insults readers who came for that source  _(5)_

**On the prose itself**
- Quotability is a benchmark: readers hunt for a line to share and mark down books where nothing sparked  _(6)_
- Verbal tics get counted: sentences all starting with the name, stacked lists, alliterative synonyms, random Capitalized Importance  _(4)_
- 'Lifeless' prose, where the reader hears the scene but does not feel it, sinks otherwise bold plots  _(4)_
- Editing lapses show: grammar slips, continuity contradictions and dated pop-culture name-drops pull readers out  _(4)_
- Dense, challenging prose is accepted when rereading a sentence repays; unanchored invented terms and jargon lose readers instead  _(3)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- An opening set piece or in-media-res hook that 'blows the doors off' earns goodwill readers spend for hundreds of pages  _(14)_
- Readers grant 100-150 slow pages if the world or voice promises payoff; many say 'give it time, it hooks you eventually'  _(10)_
- A strong first act that the plot then abandons reads as bait-and-switch and angers more than a weak start does  _(7)_
- A flood of unfamiliar names and terms is tolerated when context makes them clear within a few chapters; otherwise confusion sours  _(5)_
- One repugnant early moment, such as body-shaming or coercion played as sexy, can end the book at 10%  _(3)_

**Middle**
- Travel, quest slogs and war-preparation stretches are where readers start skimming; the sagging middle is the most named weak spot  _(8)_
- Repeated cycles (same fight, same plan talk, same self-doubt) make readers feel they are rereading; vary the beats  _(7)_
- A second half that 'saves the book' is common praise, but readers rate the whole, so front-load some payoff  _(5)_
- When sidekicks or background threads outshine the main plot, readers notice and call the main story thin  _(3)_
- A mid-book set piece as gripping as a finale is loved, though it can make the actual ending feel flatter  _(3)_

**Ending**
- Rushed, convenient wrap-ups, miracle rescues and bows on every subplot are the most frequent ending complaint  _(18)_
- Mysteries made a big deal of must pay off; leaving core questions unanswered leaves readers feeling cheated  _(9)_
- A series finale must tie back to the first book and honor who the characters have been; betraying them 'rings false'  _(7)_
- Cliffhangers are relished when the book delivered; when it didn't, a cliffhanger last line comes 'too late' to make readers care  _(7)_
- A last 15-25% that is breathtaking redeems a slow build; readers stay up till dawn and forgive the drag  _(6)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**dialogue** `dialogue`
- Banter among a crew or between partners is the most quoted element in rave reviews; witty exchanges sell the book  _(8)_
- Profanity or modern slang dropped in to feel adult or edgy reads as unnecessary and breaks period continuity  _(3)_
- Characters sitting down to discuss events the reader just witnessed is called 'talking it to death'  _(2)_
- Snark that is announced but doesn't land is mocked; forced zingers hurt more than no humor at all  _(2)_

**action** `action`
- Named set pieces (a heist, a fencing bout, a chase, a long con) are what readers remember and list by name  _(11)_
- Frantic sequences where readers can't tell what is happening are a stated flaw even in otherwise liked books  _(3)_
- Nonstop action with no emotional anchor leaves readers unattached to the hero until three-quarters in  _(2)_

**intimacy** `intimacy`
- Love declared after a day of acquaintance, with the plot stopping for it, is condemned across YA and adult titles alike  _(19)_
- Slow-burn chemistry built on banter, opposites and resistance is praised; readers swoon at tension, not speed  _(10)_
- Sex scene after sex scene in place of plot or world-building drops the rating even when readers call them scorching  _(5)_
- Coercion, possessiveness or sexual demands framed as romance trigger DNFs and outrage  _(4)_

**exposition** `exposition`
- Every creature type and subplot in one book means none makes an impact; readers say 'take off the last three accessories'  _(10)_
- Pages of history or science info-dump bury plot and character; readers skim and lose the thread  _(5)_
- Interleaved flashback or origin chapters that feed the present storyline are praised for depth and payoff  _(5)_
- Readers want the why behind a character's damage or a villain's drive, not just the fact of it 'tossed out there'  _(4)_

**quiet** `quiet`
- Reflective passages earn their place when they deepen a character; repeated self-questioning on the same theme gets skimmed  _(5)_
- Domestic routine narrated at length (cooking, cleaning, errands, hair) is the loudest 'nothing happens' complaint  _(3)_

## General & literary fiction
<a id="general"></a>

**Key:** `general` · **Reviews:** 190 · **Matches genre labels like:** literary, literary fiction, contemporary fiction, general fiction, fiction, drama, family saga, domestic, magical realism, satire, …

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Characters who feel like real people, complex and flawed and understood from inside, are the single most-praised element  _(24)_
- Readers prize being made to feel: tears, heartbreak, a book that haunts them for weeks after the last page  _(18)_
- Clean, restrained prose that only occasionally floors it beats constant flourish; 'nothing superfluous' is high praise  _(17)_
- Forward pull matters even in literary fiction: short chapters, tightening connections, 'couldn't put it down'  _(15)_
- Humor and warmth threaded through serious material earn affection; 'laughed and cried' is the ideal reaction  _(13)_
- A setting rendered so specifically it becomes a character is remembered even after the plot fades  _(13)_
- An ending that feels inevitable yet unforeseen, paying off earlier setups, retroactively elevates the whole book  _(13)_
- Fiction that makes readers interrogate their own lives and choices is valued over fiction that answers for them  _(11)_

**Readers punish**
- Flat, cardboard, or distant characters the reader 'feels nothing' for is the top complaint, worse than unlikeability  _(24)_
- Endings that are rushed, tacked-on, cop-out ambiguous, or wrapped too neatly cost a book one to two stars  _(17)_
- Whiny, privileged protagonists with low real stakes exhaust patience; readers end up siding with minor characters  _(16)_
- Heavy-handed messages, agendas, or lessons hammered home; readers resent being preached at or manipulated  _(14)_
- Fragmented structure, sprawling casts, and constant time-jumps that cut scenes short dilute investment  _(14)_
- Overwritten prose (metaphor pileups, adjective overload, 25-cent words) reads as pretension or trying too hard  _(13)_
- Implausible behavior: capable characters act stupidly or out of character because the plot requires it  _(12)_
- Formula, retreads, and telegraphed reveals; readers spot a trademark device on its second use and call it  _(11)_

**They come in expecting**
- Acclaim is a liability: readers measure prizewinners against the hype and punish 'emperor's new clothes' harshly  _(15)_
- Beautiful sentences do not excuse plotlessness; 'great writer, no story' is the archetypal three-star verdict  _(10)_
- Ambiguity must be earned: an open ending after real convergence passes, one that dodges resolution is a cheat  _(8)_
- Melodrama, movie-of-the-week plotting, and soap-opera coincidence count as cheating in a book presented as literary  _(8)_
- Dark or unlikeable is acceptable, readers say so explicitly, provided the darkness serves truth rather than shock  _(7)_

**On the prose itself**
- Readers hunt for quotable lines; a few true sentences redeem a mixed book but cannot carry a dead one  _(11)_
- Restraint is the compliment: describe just enough, remove one accessory, let five percent of pages carry the lyricism  _(8)_
- Telling in summary ('this happened, then this') flattens voice; readers want scenes they can inhabit  _(8)_
- Repeating one point, verb tic, or physical detail is noticed by the third instance and resented by the tenth  _(7)_
- Dialogue must sound like people: kids like kids, spouses not reciting facts they both already know  _(6)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- A strong opening the book then squanders is the most frequent two-to-three-star story; the promise raises the bar  _(9)_
- Readers judge the voice within a chapter; overwriting or manipulation on page one ends the book around page 55  _(7)_
- Hooking fast works (an early tragedy, a vivid first-page premise), but readers grant patience if warned it pays off  _(6)_
- Opening on a death then settling into family study frustrates readers primed for a mystery; signal the mode early  _(3)_

**Middle**
- The middle sags: wheel-spinning, lagging, 'the second half went stagnant' recur; slack is forgiven only if it pays off  _(9)_
- Side characters' backstories and subplots introduced mid-book read as sidetracks from the story readers signed up for  _(6)_
- Mid-book is where readers want connections to tighten and threads to start converging, not multiply  _(4)_
- Hammering the same theme in incident after incident reads as repetition rather than development  _(4)_

**Ending**
- A landing stuck cleanly, inevitable yet surprising and earned, is the stated reason for five stars over four  _(12)_
- Last-20% collapse is a named phenomenon: rushed resolution, deus ex machina, the tide turning for the good guys  _(8)_
- Unresolved endings split readers; they pass only when the convergence has already happened on the page  _(8)_
- Cheap late twists and plot-device reveals retroactively cheapen everything that came before  _(4)_
- Too-neat happy endings for messy material read as a bow tied on a soap opera  _(4)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**dialogue** `dialogue`
- Dialogue that rings true, children who talk like children and banter that lands, is singled out for praise  _(4)_
- Characters explaining what they both know, or a narrator interrupting speech to explain, break the scene  _(3)_

**action** `action`
- Suspense works when escalation feels like actual human behavior under pressure, not plot necessity  _(3)_
- Brisk pacing buys forgiveness for borrowed tropes and flat types; readers enjoy the ride if it never slows  _(3)_

**intimacy** `intimacy`
- Explicit sex that reveals no character reads as porn or comedy; readers say the story was stronger without it  _(4)_
- The affair-as-self-discovery that circles back to the spouse is a tired device readers now call out  _(3)_

**exposition** `exposition`
- Infodumps and characters lecturing bystanders about the world are called lazy even by readers who liked the book  _(6)_
- Readers like meeting a strange world halfway: told just enough to orient, never bewildered, never spoon-fed  _(3)_

**quiet** `quiet`
- Patient accumulation of small disappointments in restrained prose can devastate more than catastrophe  _(5)_
- Interior stretches with no pressure behind them read as aimless and get skimmed  _(4)_

## Historical fiction
<a id="historical"></a>

**Key:** `historical` · **Reviews:** 190 · **Matches genre labels like:** historical, historical fiction, period, wartime, world war, ww2, wwii, victorian, medieval, tudor, …

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Lived-in period detail (cold, food, squalor, work) earns trust; readers sense research woven into scene rather than displayed  _(20)_
- Characters who change and whom readers grow to love; crying over them is the most-cited reason for five stars  _(19)_
- A distinctive narrative voice or device (odd narrator, dry wit, charm) carries readers through slow or grim stretches  _(13)_
- Propulsive plotting: short chapters, a mission or mystery, cliffhangers; 'read it in one day' is top praise  _(13)_
- Teaching a forgotten corner of history (a ship, a trial, a colony) through story, not lecture, is a core pleasure  _(11)_
- Fierce, goal-driven protagonists, especially women with 'gumption', hold readers even when the plot sags  _(11)_
- Multiple POVs or timelines are rewarded when the strands visibly converge or echo each other by the end  _(10)_
- Dark material balanced by kindness, humor or hope: readers want to be moved, not traumatized  _(9)_

**Readers punish**
- Flat or opaque characters: 'I never got to know her' is the top complaint and sinks even well-researched books  _(27)_
- Bloat: excess description, tangents, repeated scenes; readers say 'needed an editor' and estimate pages to cut  _(24)_
- Coincidences, nick-of-time rescues and piled-on misfortunes: melodrama replaces the empathy readers came for  _(16)_
- Romance that romanticizes coercion, rape or controlling husbands; readers DNF in rage, even fans of the author  _(14)_
- Plodding middles with no declared stakes; readers 'wait and wait' then quit between 40 and 65 percent  _(14)_
- Repetition of phrases, scenes or tics (dialect words, catchphrases, the same mistake) becomes a drinking game  _(12)_
- Flattening real historical figures into harpies or saints; readers are protective of maligned women and check the record  _(11)_
- Casts too large to track: dozens introduced early then dropped, or linked stories that never let you know anyone  _(10)_

**They come in expecting**
- Readers assume the facts of real events are right and will look them up; errors in chronology or custom disqualify  _(10)_
- The era must shape behavior; anachronistic language or uniformly modern, tolerant attitudes read as 'in costume'  _(9)_
- In dual-timeline books the contemporary strand must earn its place; it is usually the weaker one and gets skimmed  _(9)_
- Holocaust, slavery and WWII are held to a higher bar: thrill-seeking or sentimental use of atrocity reads as exploitation  _(8)_
- An author's note on sources and departures from fact buys goodwill, even from critical readers  _(5)_

**On the prose itself**
- Lyrical prose is prized, but 'showing off', purple metaphor and stock phrases ('scared to death') break immersion  _(13)_
- Characters who orate: speeches carrying the author's politics, or dialogue that explains history to the reader  _(8)_
- Stating emotions outright ('he felt jealous') or pages of 'thought passages' with no dialogue insult the reader  _(6)_
- Unmarked shifts of time, place or speaker (including ambiguous pronouns) force rereading and breed resentment  _(6)_
- Dialect and foreign words: a light touch adds authenticity; dense phonetic brogue or untranslated terms wear thin  _(6)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- Strong openings that collapse: 'it started off engagingly' is the refrain of two-star reviews; keep the promise you make  _(10)_
- A slow start is forgiven if the book finds its rhythm by the one-third mark  _(7)_
- Openings that introduce dozens of names, or jump time and place without transition, lose readers before chapter five  _(7)_
- A first paragraph with a striking voice or a fairy-tale hook is quoted back with love  _(5)_
- Shocking events before the reader knows the character land only cerebrally  _(3)_

**Middle**
- Middles that repeat (same scene, same scrape, same meal) or stall are where most DNFs happen  _(12)_
- Readers quit mid-book when no arc has declared itself: 'at 40 percent they were still on the road'  _(9)_
- Switching protagonist or letting a vivid secondary character fade mid-book leaves readers disconnected  _(6)_
- Fitting in every research find (botany, battles, bowel movements) mid-book reads as self-indulgence  _(5)_

**Ending**
- A moving ending redeems much: critics concede 'the ending is genuinely well managed'; strong last 50 pages lift ratings  _(10)_
- Rushed or abrupt endings after a long build ('thirty pages missing', 'felt cheated') are the top ending complaint  _(9)_
- Reveals already hinted at, or foreseeable from the first cough, make the whole journey feel wasted  _(7)_
- Late genre swerves (mystical bear, love saves the day, sudden bloodbath) after a realistic book feel like betrayal  _(7)_
- Forgiveness granted too quickly to a hero who wronged the heroine sours the whole romance  _(5)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**dialogue** `dialogue`
- Witty sparring and name-calling between leads is quoted and savored  _(4)_
- Two-page speeches voicing the author's agenda make characters 'orate, not talk'  _(3)_

## Mystery, thriller & crime
<a id="mystery-thriller-crime"></a>

**Key:** `mystery_thriller_crime` · **Reviews:** 190 · **Matches genre labels like:** mystery, thriller, crime, noir, detective, suspense, police procedural, whodunit, spy, espionage, …

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- A reveal readers didn't see coming that still makes sense when they rewind is the single most-praised element  _(23)_
- Readers invest in a lead who is capable but visibly flawed; having someone to root for carries the dark material  _(13)_
- Setting that does suspense work (bleak landscape, closed town, sinister house) gets named as a strength in itself  _(12)_
- Factual 'payload' (history, forensics, procedure, newsroom craft) is a draw when it rides inside the chase  _(12)_
- Sustained unease from the first pages, tension that 'never lets up', is what earns 'couldn't put it down'  _(10)_
- Short chapters that each end on a turn make readers read 'one more' and forgive other weaknesses  _(7)_
- Sections inside the killer's head (diaries, POV chapters) are singled out as the most chilling parts  _(7)_
- Graphic violence is accepted, even praised, when it visibly serves the story rather than exists to shock  _(6)_

**Readers punish**
- A cast nobody cares about kills the stakes; readers stop caring who lives or dies and rate it 1-2 stars  _(25)_
- A killer guessable before the halfway mark sinks the rating no matter how readable the rest is  _(20)_
- Books that lose steam at 50-60% draw 'could have been 100-150 pages shorter'; filler is noticed and named  _(19)_
- Romance, instalove or a love triangle crowding out the mystery is the most common 'ruined it for me' complaint  _(17)_
- Protagonists who never call the police, trust strangers in a day, or ignore obvious tools read as stupid  _(16)_
- Convenient memory lapses, tech-as-explanation and plot-serving coincidence read as the author stalling or cheating  _(12)_
- Twists chosen to shock, that contradict character or logic, provoke anger rather than awe  _(10)_
- Ambiguous endings, cliffhangers in standalones and 'no answers' feel like being cheated of the payoff  _(8)_

**They come in expecting**
- Series readers expect leads to grow; recycled gags and a repeated formula read as phoned in  _(10)_
- If it is sold as a thriller, something thrilling must happen; a character study in a thriller jacket gets punished  _(9)_
- Unlikeable leads are fine if compelling; an antihero works, an unlikeable AND dull cast does not  _(6)_
- Fair play matters: readers rewind to find the clues and feel cheated if they were never there  _(6)_
- A proper mystery needs several plausible suspects; one obvious candidate makes it suspense, not mystery  _(5)_

**On the prose itself**
- Itemized driving routes, meals, jogging paths and daily routines are read as padding, not texture  _(8)_
- A few sharp, near-poetic lines in a thriller get quoted and praised; readers notice above-par prose  _(7)_
- Witty, dark-humored banter is a draw; forced quirk, teen-speak or pages of aimless talk reads contrived  _(7)_
- Overwritten, 'literary' pretension in a suspense novel reads as trying too hard  _(5)_
- Plain prose is forgiven if it moves; dry prose with no atmosphere leaves readers unable to care  _(5)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- A slow start is tolerated as a genre norm so long as a sinister undertone is present from page one  _(9)_
- A first chapter that 'blows me away' buys goodwill that carries readers through later flaws  _(7)_
- When the blurb's hook event arrives past the midpoint, or after 20% of backstory, readers feel cheated  _(5)_
- Opening on the aftermath (a death already happened, a survivor's account) hooks immediately  _(4)_

**Middle**
- The middle is where books 'go downhill'; the second half must escalate, not pad toward a wrap-up  _(19)_
- Everything-but-the-kitchen-sink subplots added mid-book to stay twisty read as clutter  _(5)_
- Investigations that loop (same searching, suspect to suspect, no new information) exhaust readers  _(5)_
- A mid-book escalation or genre shift (mystery into psychological thriller) re-hooks and is welcomed  _(4)_

**Ending**
- A guessed reveal defines the rating; readers sort endings into surprising, so-so, or 'saw it miles away'  _(20)_
- Leaving the central question unanswered reads as lazy, not artful; readers want closure  _(8)_
- A climax too crammed or confusing to follow evaporates the emotional payoff built before it  _(4)_
- Epilogues that over-explain, leap decades ahead or turn saccharine undercut an otherwise earned finish  _(4)_
- A final sting on the last page, after things seem wrapped up, is remembered as 'a cracker'  _(4)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**dialogue** `dialogue`
- Dialogue that sounds written to seem young or quirky reads fake; readers cringe  _(4)_
- Madcap, dark-humored partner banter is praised as the fun of a detective tale  _(3)_
- Pages of talk without action or stakes read as filler: 'too much talk and not enough action'  _(3)_

**action** `action`
- Action lands only when readers are unsure the hero will make it and care who might die  _(4)_
- Strings of chases and fights with little connecting them numb rather than thrill  _(3)_
- A climax confusing to follow, or 'ham-fisted' in tying threads, is where ratings drop  _(3)_

**intimacy** `intimacy`
- Erotic-suspense readers grade sexual tension and sex scenes separately from plot; both must hold  _(7)_
- Romance told rather than shown, or instant, reads artificial; slow-burn attraction gets credit  _(6)_
- Sex irrelevant to the plot, or abuse framed as desire, draws the angriest one-star reviews  _(5)_

**exposition** `exposition`
- Historical, technical or procedural detail is a draw when woven into the pursuit, not lectured  _(8)_
- Piling on explanation to make a conspiracy convincing backfires; readers either go along or don't  _(4)_
- Backstory summarized in a few sentences, or front-loaded before the premise event, reads lazy  _(4)_

**quiet** `quiet`
- Long stretches inside one obsessive narrator's head exhaust; flashbacks or other POVs give relief  _(4)_
- Character study replacing the plot that hooked the reader drains the thrill  _(4)_
- Interior passages earn their place when the voice is sharp or the observation rings true  _(4)_

## Romance
<a id="romance"></a>

**Key:** `romance` · **Reviews:** 190 · **Matches genre labels like:** romance, romantic, love story, romantic comedy, rom-com, contemporary romance, historical romance, paranormal romance, regency, second chance, …

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Humor and quick banter between the leads is the most-cited pleasure; it buys forgiveness for thin plots  _(24)_
- Chemistry that reads as connection, not just lust; readers call it 'palpable' and rate the book on it  _(20)_
- Readers want to be wrecked: tears and 'I felt everything' are five-star language; emotional stakes beat plot stakes  _(20)_
- Protective, possessive hero wins when he is also patient, vulnerable and devoted; worship lands, domination alone does not  _(15)_
- Heroines who hold their ground, say what they think and don't whine are singled out by name for praise  _(15)_
- Steam lands when balanced with raw emotion; 'heat, heart, humor' is the formula readers state outright  _(13)_
- A warm secondary cast (family, friends, prior-book couples) is a selling point readers return for, not filler  _(13)_
- A fresh spin on a familiar trope (virgin hero, caveman POV, nerd Cinderella) is what readers remember and reward  _(12)_

**Readers punish**
- An immature, indecisive or self-absorbed heroine sinks the book even when the hero is adored  _(24)_
- Readers count pages: 400+ for a simple arc, repeated scenes and 'nothing happens' stretches draw the harshest ratings  _(16)_
- Hero cruelty, sleeping around under her nose, or ignoring 'no' is not redeemed by a late apology  _(15)_
- Break-up/make-up loops and withheld-information misunderstandings read as padding, not conflict  _(15)_
- Trope checklists with no personal twist get called 'cookie cutter'; readers name the book it copies  _(15)_
- Typos, run-ons and repeated tics (stares, snorts, lip-biting, pet names) get literally tallied in reviews  _(15)_
- Insta-love with no visible build-up is the top structural complaint: 'I must have missed the tension'  _(14)_
- Being told the couple has a connection instead of shown one: 'as much chemistry as pieces of furniture'  _(14)_

**They come in expecting**
- A real ending is owed; cliffhangers, 'standalone' serials and books that stop at 45% feel like a scam  _(9)_
- Alpha is wanted, abuse is not: a possessive hero must never cross into coercion or steamroll consent  _(8)_
- The love story must carry the book; suspense, sport or sequel-couple setup that crowds it out is penalized  _(6)_
- Cover, title and blurb are a contract; a promised premise (nude resort, 30-day pact) that never arrives angers  _(6)_
- Stacked trauma (abuse, rape, dead parents) deployed as an emotional shortcut is seen through and resented  _(5)_

**On the prose itself**
- Readers tally repeated gestures and words; one tic used 80+ times becomes the whole review  _(7)_
- Sappy declarations ('I die when you blink') and endless 'Angel' endearments read as cringe, not swoon  _(6)_
- Run-ons, typos and missing edits make a book feel like a draft; readers quit by 10% and say so  _(6)_
- An addictive, un-put-down-able voice carries readers through stories they dislike, and they admit it  _(6)_
- Sex-scene euphemisms (petals, channel, back passage, musky) break the mood and get quoted mockingly  _(5)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- Readers report being hooked by chapter one; a first page that sparks is cited as the reason for five stars  _(10)_
- A riveting first 20-30% that then sags is a common two-star shape: 'the start was great, then...'  _(10)_
- A slow or odd first 100 pages is forgiven if the midpoint finally delivers swoon  _(5)_
- A memorable meet-cute (stranger in the bathtub, elevator, mistaken arrest) is recalled and praised  _(5)_

**Middle**
- The middle is where 'draggy' happens: snail pace, conversations rehashing what we just read, outfit lists  _(9)_
- Serial break-ups and make-ups across the middle are experienced as angst padding  _(5)_
- Consummation timing matters: at 89% it is 'blue balls'; at 52% with no build it feels random  _(5)_
- A midpoint shift that finally delivers swoon can rescue a disliked first half  _(4)_
- When the sequel couple or the mystery takes over the middle, readers notice the lead romance thinning  _(3)_

**Ending**
- Cliffhangers and abrupt non-endings are the most-cited ending failure, even from fans of the author  _(9)_
- HEA payoff plus an epilogue is loved; readers routinely wish the epilogue were longer  _(8)_
- A mystery solved in the last five pages or redemption arriving 'too late' reads as anticlimactic  _(8)_
- A non-rosy ending is accepted when it fits; readers call it 'the right ending for this story'  _(4)_
- Grand-gesture finales (limo, ring, dream dress) ring hollow unless the relationship earned them  _(4)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**dialogue** `dialogue`
- Bickering 'down to an art' and witty exchanges are the scenes readers quote back in reviews  _(7)_
- Declaration scenes tip into cringe when the lines go purple; readers quote them to mock  _(4)_
- Heavy phonetic brogue or accent in dialogue wears thin fast  _(2)_

**action** `action`
- Suspense works when believable and proportionate; serial kidnappings or a one-page villain wrap-up do not  _(7)_

**intimacy** `intimacy`
- Steamy scenes are praised when they carry emotion and the couple's dynamic, not just mechanics  _(10)_
- Anatomy euphemisms and 'devoured' mouths pull readers out; plain, confident heat is preferred  _(6)_
- Heat level must match the premise: fade-to-black in a sex-addict story or a chaste 'dirty' book disappoints  _(6)_
- Sex with no prior tension reads as random; readers ask 'where did that come from?'  _(5)_

**exposition** `exposition`
- World and setting woven through the romance (a city, folklore, shifter rules) are praised as immersive  _(6)_
- A paranormal or sports world that is barely used or left vague feels like a missed layer  _(4)_
- Recapping prior books or retelling the same story from another POV reads as filler  _(3)_
- Past/present timelines must feel emotionally different or the switching exhausts  _(3)_

**quiet** `quiet`
- Repeated interior loops (horniness, insecurity, self-pity) in introspective stretches drive readers off  _(6)_
- Long domestic stretches (cooking, cleaning, wedding planning, outfits) are called out as page filler  _(4)_
- Slow, artful interior shifts (a prejudice unwinding, journal entries) are praised when they carry change  _(3)_

## Young adult
<a id="young-adult"></a>

**Key:** `young_adult` · **Reviews:** 190 · **Matches genre labels like:** young adult, ya, teen, coming-of-age, coming of age, high school, new adult, middle grade

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Fast pacing and short chapters are the most-cited virtue; 'read it in one sitting' is the highest praise and rescues flawed books  _(28)_
- Flawed, honest, even unpleasant heroines who feel like real people earn devotion; readers want to argue with them, not admire them  _(24)_
- Friendship, sibling and family bonds often land harder than the romance; readers name them as the best element  _(20)_
- Wit and banter from the narrator carry light books; humor is what makes shallow plots forgivable  _(18)_
- Twists still land when readers half-guess them; the pleasure is being 'shook' despite predicting, not being fooled  _(18)_
- World-building readers can picture clearly is savored; they want to 'crawl inside' a world that feels bigger than the plot  _(18)_
- Beautiful prose buys forgiveness: readers finish confusing or slow books purely for the sentences and say so  _(16)_
- Slow-burn romance where the pair earn trust first produces breath-holding payoff; readers call the wait worth it  _(15)_

**Readers punish**
- 'I've read this book a hundred times': stock plots and cliché sets are the top complaint, named item by item  _(29)_
- Cardboard secondary characters or too many names; readers stop caring when deaths don't register  _(29)_
- Slow stretches of filler, repeated thoughts and 'could have been cut in half' drive DNFs  _(24)_
- Whiny, self-centered or stupid heroines who act recklessly for plot reasons are despised, not forgiven  _(22)_
- Contrived coincidences, deus ex machina and choices no real person would make break trust fast  _(20)_
- Romance that swallows a sci-fi, fantasy or mystery plot is resented; readers praise books that keep it subordinate  _(19)_
- Hazy world-building: naming places is not building them; unexplained magic or premise leaves readers lost  _(15)_
- Insta-love is a near-universal pet peeve, forgiven only when prose and character depth are exceptional  _(12)_

**They come in expecting**
- Love triangles are tolerated only when they make strategic or emotional sense; gratuitous ones are an eye-roll  _(14)_
- Retellings must stay recognizable yet twist; distorting the source's heart or renaming myths insults fans  _(11)_
- Heavy topics (mental illness, abuse, grief, race) must feel researched and authentic; readers can tell when they aren't  _(10)_
- Diversity is welcomed but not enough alone; a queer or POC character given no arc of their own reads as token  _(10)_
- Contemporary YA is expected to be realistic; film-style coincidences and parents who don't react count as cheating  _(8)_

**On the prose itself**
- Simplistic, 'standard' or repetitive prose is noticed and marked down even when the plot holds  _(11)_
- Jokey tone, slang or profanity in a dark story drops the intensity; writing that feels too young for brutal content jars  _(10)_
- A comparison in every paragraph or a figure of speech in every line reads as trying too hard  _(6)_
- Teen dialogue must sound like actual teens; robotic flirting or ornate speech kills believability  _(6)_
- A narrator who performs for the reader or seems aware of being read feels inauthentic  _(2)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- Openings that take 100+ pages to engage lose readers to DNF; slowness is tolerated only with a strong voice  _(10)_
- Hooks that land in the first sentence or first five pages are remembered and quoted  _(6)_
- A strong first third that then 'does a 180' into clichés angers readers more than a weak start  _(6)_
- Characters introduced early then dropped from the plot are missed and resented  _(4)_

**Middle**
- Repetitive middles—endless walking, riding, parties, the same confrontation again—are where books sag  _(9)_
- Readers often say the last third was great and 'should have been like that all along'  _(5)_
- Romance kicking in mid-book and taking over derails readers who came for the plot  _(4)_
- Alternating past/present chapters that reveal a crew's backstory are praised for pacing  _(3)_

**Ending**
- Earned, honest conclusions—even upsetting ones—are praised; 'no sugar-coating' is a compliment  _(9)_
- Predictable endings are accepted in contemporary romance if the journey was fun  _(7)_
- Rushed, hazy or tone-breaking final chapters can sink an otherwise liked book  _(6)_
- Cliffhangers and unanswered questions read as 'a third of a book' and feel like a cheat  _(6)_
- Resolutions that come too easily—a five-minute talk, a regime toppled offstage—feel unearned  _(6)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**dialogue** `dialogue`
- Awkward-sweet flirting exchanges are singled out as highlights  _(5)_
- Dialogue must sound like real teens; stilted or over-figurative talk breaks it  _(4)_
- Banter in the middle of danger reads as childish and deflates stakes  _(3)_

**action** `action`
- Peril the reader knows will pass is skimmed; danger must feel like it could cost something  _(4)_
- Fight scenes earn praise when clearly staged and easy to visualize  _(4)_
- Readers want action early and recurring; long walking stretches between set pieces kill momentum  _(4)_

**intimacy** `intimacy`
- Romantic payoff after a long build produces the breath-holding scenes readers remember  _(5)_
- Attraction rooted in looks alone is a turn-off; show why they fall  _(4)_
- Queer romances kept chaste or off-page frustrate readers who want to feel it  _(4)_
- Groping or sex scenes near trauma read as gross and cheap  _(2)_

**exposition** `exposition`
- Both over-explaining to convince and under-explaining the premise are punished; aim for clarity without pleading  _(6)_
- Visible research (illness, prostitution, war, culture) earns trust; shallow cultural detail disappoints  _(6)_
- Backstory that is told, not witnessed, carries no emotion  _(4)_
- Didactic lectures on themes readers already accept slow the book  _(3)_

**quiet** `quiet`
- Interior rumination that repeats the same thought is skimmed or DNF'd  _(6)_
- Quiet, melancholic books work when voice and prose carry them: 'calm until it isn't'  _(4)_
