# Reader Lens — what readers reward and punish, by genre

_Generated 2026-11-09 from **8,330** reader reviews. Source: UCSD Book Graph (Goodreads reviews, Wan & McAuley) — 1,190 reviews per genre (4×250 mapped by Claude agents + a 190-review first pass), balanced across star ratings, weighted by helpful votes, ≤2 per book; reduced per genre._

Every line below is **guidance, not a rule**: it describes what readers of a genre reward or punish in their own reviews, so the AI author can deviate deliberately rather than by accident. The number after each line is the evidence count — how many reviews in the sample supported it. Scene kinds are **specific to each genre**, discovered from what its readers actually discuss; the planner tags each section with one or two of them and the writer receives that scene's guidance.

## Erotica & erotic romance
<a id="erotica"></a>

**Key:** `erotica` · **Reviews:** 1,190 · **Matches genre labels like:** erotica, erotic, erotic romance, bdsm, kink, steamy, explicit romance, adult romance, smut, dark romance

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Humor and quick banter woven through the heat is the most-cited reason a steamy book becomes a favourite  _(92)_
- A suspense, mystery or revenge thread with twists readers don't see coming keeps pages turning between intimate scenes  _(85)_
- Sexual tension and anticipation before consummation is rated as highly as the sex itself, often higher  _(82)_
- A possessive or commanding hero lands when tenderness, humor or visible care shows beneath the dominance  _(81)_
- A heroine with backbone who challenges, outwits or calls out the hero is praised nearly as often as he is  _(74)_
- Emotional stakes that leave readers wrecked or in tears earn five stars even when the sex is secondary  _(72)_
- A damaged or dark hero readers empathize with against their judgment, his humanity glimpsed in layers, is treasured  _(66)_
- Vivid secondary characters and friend groups enrich the world and sell the next book  _(42)_

**Readers punish**
- A doormat, whining or too-stupid-to-live heroine is the single most common reason for one- and two-star reviews  _(124)_
- Sex standing in for plot: when every beat ends in bed, readers skim even the scenes they came for and call it plotless  _(97)_
- A hero who lies, stalks, degrades or controls without tenderness or consequence reads as abuser, not alpha  _(97)_
- Billionaire-dominant setups echoing famous titles are dismissed as knock-offs unless something new is on the table  _(75)_
- Mechanical, checklist intimate scenes with no felt chemistry are the most damning complaint however explicit they are  _(67)_
- Love declared after one encounter or a couple together by chapter three robs readers of watching them fall  _(62)_
- A hero touching another woman after meeting her, or on-page other-woman/other-man content, reads as cheating whatever the rules  _(51)_
- One story stretched over serial installments or crammed into a thin novella reads as a cash grab with undeveloped leads  _(51)_

**They come in expecting**
- Readers grade heat and story on separate axes; hot scenes alone earn about three stars and cannot rescue a weak plot  _(114)_
- Dark, taboo or non-con content is accepted when the blurb warns for it and the author owns it; surprise or softened darkness costs stars  _(60)_
- An HEA or HFN is assumed even in dark romance; cliffhangers are tolerated only when flagged and the next part is near  _(56)_
- Readers audit 'safety' (other-woman/other-man scenes, cheating, condoms, virginity, abuse) and report it to each other  _(54)_
- Over-the-top possessive heroes get a fantasy pass as long as the tone signals fantasy and the story makes internal sense  _(22)_

**On the prose itself**
- Dirty talk specific to the character and moment is the most quoted pleasure; porn-script or sex-ed lines break the mood  _(98)_
- Repeated heat-scene words ('mine', 'whimper', 'throbbing') and stock euphemisms get counted and mocked; vary the language  _(98)_
- Witty banter and lyrical, declarative lines get highlighted and quoted; readers notice when a writer can write  _(63)_
- Dual POV with a distinct, convincing male voice is a named draw; identical voices or a hero who sounds feminine get flagged  _(42)_
- Typos, tense slips, run-ons and unmarked POV switches get the book labeled a rough draft, even in free reads  _(38)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- A sharp voice or a specific, slightly absurd hook situation buys patience; readers name the premise as why they kept going  _(41)_
- A strong, funny first quarter that collapses midway is the most common two-star arc; the hook writes a check the middle must cash  _(35)_
- Early DNFs come from the heroine's bad first impression, a juvenile voice or the hero's first cruel act before trust exists  _(28)_
- Sex in the opening pages before any character grounding earns the 'no plot' verdict for the whole book  _(15)_

**Middle**
- Momentum lost once the couple is together, with only sex and bickering between 40-70%, is the most common structural complaint  _(86)_
- The fight-makeup-sex loop as midbook structure reads as padding and is skimmed  _(41)_
- Conflict built on a misunderstanding one conversation would fix, or a secret withheld to the last pages, exhausts readers  _(38)_
- A suspense subplot carries the middle once romance settles, but the threat must develop; one that never arrives is filler  _(34)_

**Ending**
- An epilogue glimpsing the couple years ahead is expected; its absence is among the most frequent ending complaints  _(56)_
- Cliffhangers are accepted when signposted and the installment resolves its own arc; a book that feels cut in half is resented  _(52)_
- Abrupt or rushed endings, with villains dispatched easily or issues swept aside, cost a star after a long build  _(34)_
- Bittersweet or devastating endings are rewarded when earned and true to the characters; a conveniently safe one disappoints dark readers  _(24)_
- The hero's redemption and groveling must match the offense across the book; a last-chapter apology leaves the HEA reading as settling  _(21)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**Slow-burn tease, flirtation and pursuit** `slow_burn_tease`
- Prolonged circling with escalating banter, texts and talk-first flirtation is what readers rate highest and quote most  _(61)_
- A hostile, mortifying or specific, slightly absurd first meeting is remembered; a generic bar or office intro is not  _(26)_
- Forbidden setups (boss, clergy, teacher) supply sought-after heat, but the pursued must resist for believable reasons with visible guilt  _(19)_
- Each exchange must move them closer; flirtation that loops without escalating is repetitive, and pursuit past repeated refusals reads as stalking  _(12)_

**The first time together** `first_consummation`
- Inexperienced partners who perform like veterans break belief; realistic awkwardness and care from the experienced partner is praised  _(33)_
- The first consummation pays off only when the build earned it; sex within pages of meeting reads as plotless  _(30)_
- Virginity or a first-time deadline (wedding night, auction, contract) is a recurring engine readers enjoy when her behavior matches inexperience  _(15)_
- Fading to black on the promised scene, or holding the first time until the final pages, reads as a cheat in erotic romance  _(10)_
- A first time the heroine has not clearly chosen poisons the couple for many readers even if later scenes are tender  _(9)_

**Dominance, submission and kink play** `kink_power_play`
- Kink convinces when trust, negotiation, rules and aftercare are visible and about this couple; readers who know the lifestyle spot their absence  _(52)_
- Pitch the kink honestly and keep the standard: 'BDSM lite' oversold as hardcore, or a Dom who hesitates and chases, deflates the scene  _(23)_
- Dominance without negotiation, a removed safe word, or control extending to her job, phone and friends reads as assault or abuse  _(20)_
- A checklist of practices performed without passion reads clinical; harshness with no visible care for the partner loses heat  _(10)_
- Kink framed as therapy for trauma or grief draws derision  _(8)_

**Captivity, non-consent and the 'mine' claim** `captive_claiming`
- Readers accept a monstrous captor when they are inside his head, his motive holds up and a sliver of humanity shows; pure cruelty loses them  _(32)_
- Captivity stays credible only while her fear and fight do; quick surrender, or repeatedly blaming her 'traitorous body', breaks it  _(32)_
- The 'mine' claim thrills when she visibly chooses it and he matches it with his own fidelity; an unearned claim is laughed at  _(22)_
- Sex right after an assault, rescue or beating, especially framed as healing, is a hard line that reads as glorified abuse  _(16)_
- A captor whose menace evaporates early, or dark content that turns cartoonish or repeats degradation with no progression, disappoints dark readers  _(14)_

**Menage and sharing** `menage_sharing`
- Menage works when bonds run among all partners with stakes beyond the bedroom; a partner with no POV or chemistry is furniture  _(36)_
- Sharing imposed by the hero (club, friends watching, 'with his blessing') reads as 'you're not enough' and hits a hard limit  _(25)_
- Consent and chemistry must precede the scene on the page; springing group sex on the heroine or an unwarned reader alienates  _(12)_
- Set-piece group scenes are among a book's most remembered, and where over-the-top dirty talk most often tips into comedy  _(7)_

**Set-piece and public or risky encounters** `public_risky_setpiece`
- Location variety (library, office desk, elevator, car) is a named pleasure; readers single out 'the library scene' as a favourite  _(36)_
- Scenes repeating the same choreography blur and get skipped; each must shift setting, dynamic or stakes  _(24)_
- Set pieces that interrupt every plot beat stall the story; one long, detailed scene beats ten one-page quickies  _(16)_
- The heart-pounding risk must be on the page; exhibitionism with no sense of consequence reads as careless, not hot  _(7)_
- Being watched divides readers: thrilling as the couple's private risk, repellent when the hero invites others in  _(6)_

**Fight, run, chase and make-up** `makeup_loop`
- Make-up sex as the only resolution, repeated in a withdraw-run-chase loop, exhausts readers into skimming  _(19)_
- A fight ended by sex before the issue is addressed reads as the hero getting away with it; show growth between rounds  _(16)_
- Groveling must match the offense before the take-back; an instant 180 or an off-page reconciliation does not count  _(15)_
- Second-chance reunions are beloved when the shared history is shown on the page rather than asserted  _(5)_

**Aftermath, between-scenes intimacy and epilogue** `aftermath_intimacy`
- Readers want time with the couple as a couple before the epilogue; a short settled glimpse is treasured, a sugary or domesticating one grates  _(16)_
- Couples who only connect during sex are not believed as in love; readers judge the romance by what happens between scenes  _(13)_
- Tenderness after rough sex sells the hero's care; going cold or walking out after intimacy reads as cruel unless the story owns it  _(8)_
- Readers notice protection, soreness and consequences; ignoring them is flagged as the fantasy slipping  _(7)_
- The hero finally opening up is the emotional payoff that justifies his earlier coldness  _(6)_

## Fantasy, paranormal & speculative
<a id="fantasy-paranormal"></a>

**Key:** `fantasy_paranormal` · **Reviews:** 1,190 · **Matches genre labels like:** fantasy, paranormal, urban fantasy, epic fantasy, high fantasy, science fiction, sci-fi, speculative, dystopian, horror, …

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Attachment to a whole cast decides the rating; distinct people readers care about carry a book past flaws they still notice  _(129)_
- An immersive, original world readers want to live in earns forgiveness for slow or confusing stretches  _(105)_
- Lyrical prose that still flows is quoted and savored; readers stay for the writing when the plot drags  _(85)_
- Humor and banter between companions, especially mid-danger, is quoted at length and lifts a middling plot  _(74)_
- Found-family crews, sibling loyalty and fierce friendships are loved as much as, or more than, the romance  _(69)_
- Twists that blindside yet play fair are the high point; readers love kicking themselves in hindsight  _(65)_
- Relentless momentum ('read in one sitting', 'never a dull moment') earns five stars and forgives cliches  _(63)_
- Being made to cry or ache is a feature; books that hurt beautifully are rated highest and remembered  _(62)_

**Readers punish**
- 'Nothing happens' is the fatal verdict: readers skim, DNF, and name exactly how many pages should have been cut  _(161)_
- 'I didn't care who lived or died' sinks any premise; connection to characters must arrive early  _(108)_
- Unexplained or shifting world rules and magic breed confusion, not curiosity; readers want a system that clicks  _(101)_
- A whiny, passive, foolish or bratty-called-brave heroine is the top stated reason for abandoning a book  _(81)_
- Twists and plots readers call from page one kill investment and make characters who miss them look stupid  _(80)_
- Insta-love is named and rarely forgiven, in adult titles as well as YA; attraction must be earned  _(75)_
- Borrowed premises and clones of famous series are spotted instantly and rated below the original every time  _(69)_
- Casual misogyny, racism, or rape and assault used as default peril draw one-star rants and DNFs  _(44)_

**They come in expecting**
- Each installment is judged against the best book in the series; set-up-only or recycled sequels get 'second-book syndrome'  _(83)_
- Readers expect a capable heroine who thinks and fights for herself yet stays human; women as prizes draw complaint  _(74)_
- Retellings and myth-based books are checked against the source; keep the bones, add a genuine new turn  _(63)_
- A first book must resolve its own arc; 'works as a standalone' is a selling point, withholding to sell a sequel a cheat  _(53)_
- Hype, title and blurb are a promise; a missing dragon, a not-scary horror or 'merely decent' after raves is punished  _(47)_

**On the prose itself**
- Repeated pet phrases, tics, re-described outfits and overused words are counted and quoted back mockingly  _(67)_
- Dialogue makes or breaks: distinct, witty voices get quoted; cheesy, childish or soap-opera lines get mocked  _(49)_
- Telling over showing (exposition in dialogue, events summarized offstage, psychology explained) distances readers  _(48)_
- Overwrought metaphors and purple prose read as 'trying too hard'; readers notice when a metaphor makes no sense  _(33)_
- Quotability is a benchmark; readers screenshot one memorable line and mark down books where nothing sparked  _(30)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- Readers grant roughly 100-150 pages (20-35%) for a hook, then DNF; a slow start is forgiven only if the payoff comes  _(63)_
- Front-loading names, POVs, invented terms and world-building before anyone is worth caring about drives early DNFs  _(62)_
- A gripping first act that then fizzles or changes genre angers readers more than a slow start does  _(49)_
- Opening in motion (an attack, a kill, a rescue) is praised, but plunging in without grounding confuses  _(40)_
- A distinct narrator voice on the first page wins readers at once; an irritating one sinks the book by page ten  _(11)_

**Middle**
- The 30-80% stretch is where books die: travel, set-up and 'gathering the party' without present danger get skimmed  _(52)_
- Repeated cycles (captured, rescued, scheme, repeat; the same self-doubt re-asked) make busy books feel static  _(27)_
- Inner monologue and rhetorical self-questioning in place of events drag; readers skim for dialogue  _(19)_
- Filler POVs, convenience subplots dropped later, and side characters who vanish are noticed and resented  _(18)_
- 'It gets good in the last 20%' is no defense; readers who waited that long still rate the whole book low  _(12)_

**Ending**
- Rushed, too-neat wrap-ups, deus ex machina rescues and loopholes after a long build are the top ending complaint  _(72)_
- A cliffhanger is welcomed when the book's own arc resolved; when nothing resolves it reads as a sequel ploy  _(51)_
- A strong final act can lift a middling book; readers say 'the last 100 pages saved it' and rate up  _(46)_
- Core questions must be answered and every major arc honored; a few sentences for a big thread feels like betrayal  _(43)_
- Heartbreaking endings are loved when earned and inevitable; tragedy for its own sake is not  _(30)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**Final battle and the villain's comeuppance** `climactic_showdown`
- The villain must feel threatening beforehand and get theirs on the page; a fizzled or deferred reckoning drains stakes  _(25)_
- Combat is praised when choreography is crisp and easy to follow; hectic, confusing battles deflate the whole book  _(25)_
- After hundreds of pages of build-up readers expect a 'solid kaboom'; a small, offstage or summarized climax is the chief letdown  _(20)_
- Deaths in battle land only when the fighter mattered; a pile of bodies evokes nothing  _(12)_
- Repeated battle beats (hurt, last reserves, turn the tide) blur; vary stakes and tactics and allow breathing room  _(10)_

**Learning the magic: training, trials and schools** `magic_and_training`
- Training, trials and tournaments are beloved stretches when shown as rigorous and lethal, not asserted  _(25)_
- Simple, consistent rules are praised; rules that bend to suit the plot or stay vague to the end sink the book  _(21)_
- Explain powers through use and stakes, not lecture; magic talked about but never displayed is a top complaint  _(18)_
- The school or academy section is often readers' favorite part, but it must feel like a school with vivid teachers  _(14)_
- Mentor-led training is a loved beat; a hero who masters everything without struggle reads as a cheat  _(11)_

**Romance beats: first spark, kiss and sex** `romance_beat`
- Slow-burn, love-hate sparring with banter and real resistance is the romance readers quote and crave  _(50)_
- Love triangles are smelled the moment a second male appears and called a wasted subplot when the choice is obvious  _(42)_
- A love interest who commands, watches her sleep or lies 'for her safety' reads as abuser, not alpha  _(41)_
- A special first encounter that slides straight into love breaks trust; confessions fall flat without prior tension  _(39)_
- Sex that replaces plot or repeats without development is skimmed even by readers who like steam  _(36)_

**The reveal: identity, heritage, betrayal** `big_reveal`
- The best reveals let readers say 'I knew it' on some hints and 'ooh' on others; zero seeding feels like a cheat  _(25)_
- The 'who she really is' heritage reveal is a reliably loved beat and fuels series-long speculation when seeded  _(19)_
- Reveals telegraphed from the villain's first scene insult readers and make characters who miss them look dumb  _(13)_
- Reveals withheld too long, or backstory dumped at the wrap-up, frustrate rather than tantalize  _(8)_

**Death of a beloved character** `beloved_character_death`
- Killing beloved characters is accepted, even relished, when it serves the story; readers rage and keep reading  _(31)_
- A death lands only when readers had time to love the character; one dismissed in a few sentences breaks trust  _(14)_
- Resurrections, fake-out deaths and replacement love interests drain the fear from every later death  _(11)_
- Readers openly dread favorites dying and threaten to quit, then preorder the sequel  _(11)_

**The road: quest travel and companions** `journey_quest`
- Animal and creature companions (demon horses, talking dogs, dragons) steal the show and become favorite characters  _(36)_
- Travel needs incidents, close calls and banter; 'they traveled, and traveled' is the archetypal dull stretch  _(30)_
- Colorful companions met on the road are loved; those who vanish after serving their role are missed  _(13)_
- Journeys must stay tethered to the protagonist's goal; drifting between factions reads as 'no endgame'  _(5)_

**Hauntings, monsters and creepy set pieces** `haunting_horror_beat`
- Slow-built, sustained dread beats gore; eerie atmosphere is prized as a pleasure in itself  _(45)_
- Grotesque, original monsters with distinct rules are remembered; a menagerie of every creature type dilutes them all  _(28)_
- A book sold as scary must escalate past its opening creep image; 'mysterious but never scary' is the common failure  _(17)_
- Characters who ignore obvious warnings exasperate, though stupid choices are tolerated when the creep factor delivers  _(15)_
- Ghost and possession scenes work when the entity's rules stay consistent and the heroine stays competent  _(7)_

**Court politics and scheming** `court_intrigue`
- Schemes with personal stakes and real consequences are relished; war-prep and council talk with no payoff is filler  _(21)_
- Faction politics confuse when the sides are not established; court scenes need a strong voice or they read as dull  _(11)_
- Forced betrothal and arranged marriage are a staple engine; readers want the heroine to act, not wait  _(6)_
- A heroine who hides every plan from allies purely for reader shock is called frustrating  _(3)_

## General & literary fiction
<a id="general"></a>

**Key:** `general` · **Reviews:** 1,190 · **Matches genre labels like:** literary, literary fiction, contemporary fiction, general fiction, fiction, drama, family saga, domestic, magical realism, satire, …

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Characters so real readers feel they lived with them and will miss them is the single most-praised quality  _(99)_
- Books that linger for days and make readers see their own lives, marriages and regrets on the page win lasting loyalty  _(80)_
- Distinctive, controlled prose is quoted and praised on its own; restraint that occasionally flares beats constant flourish  _(70)_
- Forward pull matters even in quiet books: short chapters, tightening threads, 'couldn't put it down' forgives many flaws  _(69)_
- Earned tears over grief, illness or loss are rated up when the emotion is specific and honest, not engineered  _(68)_
- A vivid, specific sense of place is praised even by readers who disliked the book overall  _(59)_
- Humor braided with heartbreak, funny and moving at once, is singled out as a signature pleasure  _(47)_
- Big ideas and moral questions are rewarded when carried by story and character and left open, not lectured  _(36)_

**Readers punish**
- Flat, cardboard characters nobody can care about, not even dislike, is the top complaint; it sinks even well-written books  _(167)_
- Overlength and padding: readers name the page count a book deserved, say 'needed an editor', and ask 'what was the point?'  _(147)_
- Stock types, lazy stereotypes (women as trophies or shrews) and recycled or predictable plots are spotted instantly  _(104)_
- Too many characters, unmarked POV or time shifts and fragmented structure leave readers unable to track who's who  _(84)_
- Pretension and overwriting (showing off, 25-cent words, stale postmodern devices) are mocked as a facade  _(65)_
- Preaching, lectures and authors' opinions in characters' mouths read as amateurish; message must ride inside the story  _(59)_
- A whiny, self-pitying or passive protagonist loses sympathy unless readers are shown why they are that way  _(58)_
- Contrivance, coincidence and characters acting against their nature break trust; readers tally each implausibility  _(57)_

**They come in expecting**
- Hype, prizes and 'classic' status raise the bar; 'everyone loved it but me' and 'admired, not enjoyed' reviews are harsh  _(89)_
- Unlikable leads and dark material are explicitly accepted when honestly drawn and serving truth rather than shock  _(75)_
- Cover, blurb and title set a contract; 'labeled hilarious, wasn't' or 'not the book promised' costs stars  _(49)_
- New books are measured against the author's best; a retread or a changed beloved character feels like betrayal  _(34)_
- Plotless, character-driven novels are accepted, but only if the people and the voice are worth the time  _(29)_

**On the prose itself**
- Purple description, stacked metaphors, explained symbolism and repeated tics are counted line by line; restraint is the compliment  _(87)_
- Aphoristic, quotable lines get copied into reviews; give readers sentences worth keeping  _(53)_
- Dialogue and narrator voice must fit the speaker: kids like kids, no reciting shared facts, no precocious wisdom  _(48)_
- Beautiful prose alone does not rescue a book; 'great writing, but it needs more meat' is the archetypal three-star verdict  _(39)_
- Summary instead of scene, or a turning point merely reported, leaves readers outside and feeling the author skipped it  _(34)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- A brilliant first 50-100 pages the rest cannot sustain is the most common disappointment; the promise raises the bar  _(54)_
- Readers grant roughly 50-100 pages; no hook or event by then, or a grating voice, means they quit  _(42)_
- An immediately distinctive voice or vivid setting on page one 'reels them in' and buys patience for a slow middle  _(31)_
- Opening on a death or a flash-forward to the hinge event hooks, but the book must offer more than the reveal  _(17)_
- Front-loading backstory, exposition or the whole cast before the story starts costs goodwill rarely recovered  _(17)_

**Middle**
- The middle is where books drag, wander and get skimmed; the slack is forgiven only if the ending pays off  _(41)_
- Subplots, side characters and interludes introduced mid-book with no bearing on the main arc are called padding  _(27)_
- Repeating the same beat (same scrape, same solution) kills momentum; each episode needs a twist or new pressure  _(24)_
- Mid-book is where parallel strands should visibly feed each other and threads start converging, not multiplying  _(17)_
- Flashback stretches and being 'kept in the dark' with teasers curdle into resentment by halfway  _(11)_

**Ending**
- Rushed, tacked-on or random endings (deus ex machina, dream, 40-page wrap-up) retroactively cheapen everything before  _(60)_
- A strong ending that recasts the book and feels inevitable in hindsight redeems a slog and lifts four stars to five  _(55)_
- Ambiguous endings split readers; they pass only when convergence already happened on the page, not when dodging resolution  _(43)_
- A twist from nowhere or 'it was all in her head' is cheating; one readers saw a mile off deflates  _(32)_
- Everything tied neatly (unearned redemption, instant love, every lesson learned) reads as a bow on a soap opera  _(19)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**Flashbacks and alternating then/now timelines** `past_present_timeline`
- Then/now alternation is the default structure; it works when each return to the past answers a question the present just raised  _(22)_
- A past strand weaker than the present, or flashbacks that pad and delay the hook, gets skimmed: 'just get to the point'  _(19)_
- Mark every time jump; unmarked shifts, or a past strand repeating what the present already told, lose readers  _(17)_
- Memory shown as fragments or as unreliable, and flashbacks building toward a revelation rather than explaining, are praised  _(13)_
- Looking-back novels go dry when the present-day narrator stays passive and the adult frame adds nothing  _(7)_

**Death, illness and the grief that follows** `death_and_grief`
- Tragedy stacked on tragedy reads as manipulation, a 'parade of horribles'; relentless misery tips from tragic into silly  _(26)_
- An early death or accident works when the whole book is its aftermath and survivors' secrets surface slowly  _(15)_
- Grief carried in objects and habits (wet towels, a held hand, ashes in the trunk) moves readers more than declared sorrow  _(14)_
- A death lands only if readers were made to love the person first; rushing past it to the next event denies catharsis  _(12)_
- A joke slipped into a grief scene, a family coping with humor, is where readers cry and smile at once  _(11)_

**Family gathering, homecoming and the secret that surfaces** `family_gathering`
- The wake, wedding, holiday or dinner is where buried tensions surface; 'hilariously painful' when it is the hinge of the book  _(15)_
- Long-held secrets must have real consequences; ones that one honest conversation would dissolve frustrate readers  _(14)_
- Each family member's angle on the same event in their own voice is prized, provided the central loss is not diluted  _(8)_
- Estranged siblings summoned home by a parent's illness is a recognized template; it needs a twist, not petty feuds  _(7)_
- Comic set-pieces at family events are the lines readers quote back; a crowd of new faces there annoys them  _(7)_

**Everyday domestic life and the small quiet scene** `domestic_routine`
- Everyday detail needs pressure beneath it or it is 'eating, talking, hugging, shopping'; catalogs of meals and brands are drivel  _(22)_
- Routine reveals character when a quirk has pain under it; patient accumulation of small disappointments can devastate  _(14)_
- Lavishing detail on trivia while the turning point passes in a sentence inverts what readers want detail on  _(12)_
- Recognizable era detail and immersive physical labor (haying, ranch work) bond readers; details placed like props do not  _(9)_
- Small scenes between parent and child (games, the beach, bedtime) are often named the best-written pages  _(7)_

**Sex, violence and abuse on the page** `sex_and_violence`
- Sex that exists to shock or pad is called juvenile and skimmed; readers accept it when it reveals character or moves plot  _(26)_
- Graphic abuse and violence are stomached when crucial to the picture and true; readers warn others yet still rate highly  _(15)_
- Purple or clinical anatomy ('swollen crown', 'moist') is quoted to friends for laughs and undercuts a serious book  _(11)_
- Sex as self-destruction, or age-gap and trauma-rooted attachments framed as love, alienate without psychological grounding  _(11)_
- Violence through a child's or detached eyes hits hardest; gross-out fades after the first instance and serial villainy reads as melodrama  _(9)_

**The buried secret or late twist** `secret_reveal`
- A telegraphed twist deflates; it survives only if the suspense-building was the fun, never as a rushed 'oh, by the way'  _(18)_
- The best reveals recontextualize everything, feel inevitable afterward and show the reader's early guess was half the truth  _(17)_
- A twist from nowhere, 'none of this happened' or a pointless identity reveal is called cheap, especially with no foreshadowing  _(13)_
- Setup scenes planted only to explain a later twist, or a reveal that turns out ordinary after a long tease, are resented  _(6)_
- When the secret is already known or given away on page one, prose and character must carry the story  _(5)_

**The climactic confrontation or showdown** `climactic_confrontation`
- Readers wait all book for the character to finally act and say it aloud; stewing without confronting reads as gutless  _(14)_
- Give the showdown room: resolutions in a few scant pages after long buildup are the loudest complaint, though brisk is welcome  _(12)_
- A promised standoff replaced by a shock, reminiscence or secondhand report is bait and switch  _(10)_
- Set-piece disaster or violence is the scene readers remember when escalation feels like human behavior under pressure  _(10)_
- A predictable or garbled climax wastes the setup; melodrama is tolerated only as the inevitable conclusion of what came before  _(6)_

**The marriage or romance thread inside a non-romance novel** `love_and_marriage`
- Readers must see what held a marriage together, or why two people fall for each other; a kiss and two talks is not enough  _(12)_
- A spouse leaving, an affair or a 'break' is accepted readily as premise; readers judge whether the response is believable  _(11)_
- Restraint moves most: a romance that never quite happens, a dead spouse remembered, a long marriage's silent car rides  _(8)_
- The run-off, find-new-love, return-to-spouse arc and unexamined emotional cheating are called tired and demeaning  _(7)_
- Banter is why readers root for a pair; constant rows with no warmth make them ask why these two are together  _(6)_

## Historical fiction
<a id="historical"></a>

**Key:** `historical` · **Reviews:** 1,190 · **Matches genre labels like:** historical, historical fiction, period, wartime, world war, ww2, wwii, victorian, medieval, tudor, …

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Immersive period detail (smells, food, cold, labour, slang) is the most praised quality; readers say 'I felt I was there'  _(140)_
- Learning a neglected corner of history through story, not lecture; a fresh angle on a saturated period beats famous ground  _(115)_
- Characters loved like family who grow, flawed and morally complicated rather than saintly; closing the book feels like a loss  _(96)_
- Humour and wit threaded through dark material; being made to laugh and cry beats unbroken grimness  _(63)_
- Heroines who push against era constraints with competence and wit, where the pushing costs them something real  _(53)_
- Can't-put-it-down drive even in literary historicals: secrets slowly disclosed, short chapters, genuine suspense  _(52)_
- Earned devastation: tears for a loss readers saw coming; knowing the historical outcome deepens dread rather than spoiling  _(51)_
- Multi-POV or multi-timeline sagas where every strand is strong, voices are distinct and threads visibly converge  _(49)_

**Readers punish**
- Flat, static or interchangeable characters and casts too large to know: 'I never got to know her' sinks well-researched books  _(194)_
- Bloat is the top structural complaint: readers name the 100-200 pages to cut and say 'in sore need of an editor'  _(94)_
- 'Nothing happens': chapters without consequence or forward motion; 'boring' is the commonest one-star word  _(93)_
- Repetition of phrases, scenes, tics or a formula visible from previous books becomes a drinking game  _(77)_
- Melodrama: coincidences, nick-of-time rescues, piled-on misery and characters bent out of shape to serve the plot  _(76)_
- Modern attitudes or diction, factual errors and liberties with real people break the illusion; the era must constrain characters  _(72)_
- Research dumped undigested and events told rather than shown read as a history lesson 'held at arm's length'  _(70)_
- Coercive, controlling or abusive heroes framed as romantic trigger rage reviews and DNFs, however the era is invoked  _(50)_

**They come in expecting**
- Accuracy is the contract: readers fact-check and police real people; invention is accepted where the record is blank and the author owns it  _(62)_
- Romance must not swamp the history; insta-love bolted onto a war story is 'detail on a romance coat hanger'  _(44)_
- An author's note separating fact from invention is expected, read closely and can lift a rating  _(36)_
- Historical is a wrapper: the mystery, romance or gothic inside is judged by its own rules, and the blurb's promise must be kept  _(33)_
- WWII, Holocaust, Tudor and slavery fatigue is real; atrocity settings are held to a higher bar and need a visible fresh angle  _(25)_

**On the prose itself**
- Readers quote standout lines, but lyrical prose alone never carries a book: 'I can't fault the writing, but I was bored'  _(71)_
- Overwrought, purple or show-off prose (piled adjectives, mixed metaphors, stock phrases) is quoted back as 'death by poetry'  _(64)_
- Characters who orate two-page speeches carrying the author's agenda; readers feel lectured, not told a story  _(40)_
- Dialogue must ring true to era: modern banter and pseudo-archaic 'thee/thou' pastiche are equally deal-breaking  _(33)_
- A light touch of dialect or glossed foreign words adds authenticity; dense phonetic brogue sustained for a book wears thin  _(32)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- A strong opening that collapses is the commonest arc in critical reviews: 'the beginning was the best part'  _(43)_
- A slow start is forgiven if the book grips by the one-third mark and the back half clearly repays it  _(34)_
- A vivid first scene or distinctive voice hooks before page five; shock before readers know anyone lands only cerebrally  _(34)_
- Seven or more characters introduced in the first pages make readers put the book down or reach for a cast list  _(17)_
- Front-loading birth, childhood or backstory for 200 pages before the real problem starts loses readers  _(13)_

**Middle**
- The sagging middle is where DNFs happen: readers name the exact page or percent (40-50%) they quit  _(55)_
- In dual timelines the historical strand is the one readers want; a weak modern strand is dreaded or skipped  _(35)_
- Misunderstanding loops, secrets withheld and crisis-resolution cycles pad the middle; let the conversation happen  _(26)_
- Switching to a new cast mid-book, or letting a vivid secondary character fade, leaves readers disconnected  _(19)_
- Mid-book digressions (a 75-page side trip, every research find) read as self-indulgence  _(14)_

**Ending**
- Bittersweet endings true to history are preferred to tidy wrap-ups; unearned HEAs and tacked-on epilogues are resented  _(56)_
- Twists must be fair and grow from character: a reveal seen from chapter one or a late genre swerve wastes the journey  _(54)_
- A moving final quarter can lift a flawed book a star or more; readers cite the last fifty pages as the best  _(44)_
- Rushed or abrupt endings after a long build are the top ending complaint: 'I checked I hadn't missed a page'  _(37)_
- Confusing or unresolved endings are a cop-out, not realism; only ambiguity true to the period is accepted  _(32)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**Witnessing atrocity: camps, massacre, slavery, sexual violence** `atrocity_witness`
- Graphic rape or violence repeated without purpose is skimmed; a heroine romanticizing her rapist ends the book  _(25)_
- Atrocity lands through one precise particular felt in one body, not catalogues of gore  _(19)_
- Sanitizing atrocity, cartoon perpetrators or using genocide as a thriller or romance backdrop draws anger  _(15)_
- Unflinching brutality is respected as honesty; readers expect to need breaks and do not hold it against the book  _(13)_
- Horror told at a remove leaves readers 'sad like in history class'; take them into the camp  _(10)_

**Battle, siege or combat set piece** `battle_set_piece`
- Tactics, troop movements and blow-by-blow minutiae bore unless anchored to one character's stake  _(18)_
- Dramatized action with blood, clarity and characters who can plausibly die is praised even by otherwise bored readers  _(17)_
- Characters acting stupidly in danger, or violence erupting without cause, read as melodrama, not war  _(7)_
- The battle the book builds to must be staged, not summarized in two paragraphs or won by an invincible hero  _(6)_

**Court politics and royal intrigue** `court_intrigue`
- Intrigue grips when heads can roll and stakes are personal; pure politics or theology debate bores  _(14)_
- Leaving a famous crime ambiguous, with evidence against everyone, keeps readers theorizing afterwards  _(8)_
- Squabbles over minor thrones, treason spelled out in letters or villainy by caricature break belief  _(7)_
- Feasts, dress and court minutiae are savoured; five pages on a tennis match serving nothing are not  _(6)_
- The coup, trial or execution must be dramatized, not glossed in a chapter  _(3)_

**Journey, flight or expedition through hostile country** `hostile_journey`
- Cold, hunger, disease and whom to trust are antagonists readers never tire of; nature outdoes any villain  _(13)_
- Journeys need escalating peril and a deepening bond; at 40% 'still on the road' readers quit  _(13)_
- Landscape rendered with awe is often the best prose in the book; landscape without event is a slog  _(9)_
- Companions must be allowed to die; escapes that succeed via convenient hiding places drain the stakes  _(7)_
- Readers prefer the expedition thread to the drawing-room subplot and forgive rigging detail as authenticity  _(7)_

**Courtship, arranged marriage and love scenes under period rules** `romance_and_intimacy`
- Sex repeated without changing the relationship, or inserted for shock in a grim literary book, is skimmed  _(21)_
- Girls married off for debt or alliance suffocate readers compellingly when both parties' reasons fit the period  _(20)_
- Love scenes work through the couple's emotional stakes and specific desire; explicitness without connection bores  _(18)_
- Love that history will sever is the genre's engine; forge it through shared danger, not a first glance or a misunderstanding  _(17)_
- A hero's coercion in courtship is never forgiven, and forgiveness granted within pages ends the book  _(13)_

**Household routine, meals, balls and social gatherings** `domestic_and_society`
- Craft and labour (slaughter day, restoring a painting, raising a cathedral) delight when they are a character's passion  _(21)_
- Meals, gowns and errands listed without tension numb; routine rendered as meaning (a prison day) devastates  _(18)_
- Balls and dinners come alive when an outsider exposes class and prejudice, not when gowns are described  _(10)_
- A recurring ritual or comic set piece gives shape and gets quoted; wit can carry a whole domestic novel  _(9)_
- Below-stairs scenes engage once servants get inner lives; the governess with a candle is worn out unless parodied  _(5)_

**Investigation, interrogation and trial** `investigation_and_trial`
- Trials drag when procedural; they land as duels where one defiant human choice is at stake and mirror the present  _(12)_
- An obvious culprit collapses the investigation; readers finish only for the answer  _(10)_
- Readers love assembling clues alongside the sleuth; many plausible suspects keep them questioning everyone  _(9)_
- Period forensics and documentary trial formats make the reader a juror; graphic autopsy gore divides  _(9)_
- The mystery is secondary: readers stay for the investigator's inner life; suspecting all on flimsy evidence looks silly  _(7)_

**Found letter, diary or painting bridging past and present** `dual_timeline_bridge`
- The found diary or painting works when it changes the finder, not merely explains the past  _(14)_
- The past strand almost always outshines the present; keep the modern frame lean or readers skip it  _(9)_
- Crumbling house plus buried family secret is a reliable hook; cliffhangers excuse the formula  _(8)_

## Mystery, thriller & crime
<a id="mystery-thriller-crime"></a>

**Key:** `mystery_thriller_crime` · **Reviews:** 1,190 · **Matches genre labels like:** mystery, thriller, crime, noir, detective, suspense, police procedural, whodunit, spy, espionage, …

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Propulsive momentum and short, hooky chapters earn 'couldn't put it down'; readability carries even predictable books  _(127)_
- Layered, flawed characters (a capable lead with demons, a cast that walks off the page) earn praise even when the mystery is thin  _(127)_
- A vivid, menacing sense of place (closed town, island, drought, fog, period detail) is praised as a character in itself  _(94)_
- A twist that genuinely blindsides yet recontextualizes everything before it is the most-cited reason for a top rating  _(75)_
- Dark humor and sharp partner banter lift grim material; a duo with real chemistry carries a series through weak plots  _(55)_
- Dark, disturbing material is embraced when purposeful and handled with restraint; readers brag how far a good book went  _(41)_
- Authentic grounding (procedure, forensics, period, newsroom, courtroom) adds weight when woven in lightly, never lectured  _(41)_
- Books that make readers feel (tears, fear for a child, moral discomfort) rate far above cleverly plotted but cold ones  _(39)_

**Readers punish**
- A flat or unlikable cast nobody connects with sinks the book; readers stop caring who lives or dies and twists land on nothing  _(146)_
- Padding and a sagging middle are the top structural complaint: 'cut 100-200 pages', meals and routines counted, chapters skimmed  _(108)_
- Protagonists acting stupidly (go alone, never call police, trust strangers) and implausible police draw open mockery  _(93)_
- Far-fetched plotting, coincidence and twists stacked for shock make readers feel cheated rather than surprised  _(65)_
- Romance, instalove or a love triangle swamping the mystery is a frequent 'ruined it for me' deduction  _(63)_
- A culprit spotted early (sometimes by 5%) turns the rest of the book into waiting to be proved right  _(62)_
- Long-running series recycling the formula with stagnant characters read as milking the fans  _(52)_
- Too many POVs, characters or late-added suspects leave readers confused about who is speaking and uninvested  _(30)_

**They come in expecting**
- Blurbs likening the book to the genre's megahit, or promising a different book than delivered, set readers up to punish it  _(66)_
- Labelled thriller means thrills: a quiet domestic drama or long stretches where nothing happens get 'this is not a thriller'  _(55)_
- Fair play: clues planted for hindsight and several credible suspects; one obvious candidate is suspense, not mystery  _(33)_
- Pulp, beach-read escapism is a contract readers accept happily as long as the book owns it and doesn't pretend to be more  _(25)_
- Readers prefer to go in blind and police spoilers fiercely; a blurb that gives away the first half drains the opening  _(24)_

**On the prose itself**
- Lean, precise prose and short chapters ending on a turn are felt as pace; a few quotable lines lift a rating  _(60)_
- Dialogue must sound like distinct people; witty, regionally flavored talk is a top compliment, tin-eared lines get quoted  _(38)_
- Overwrought similes, thesaurus words, typos and clunky sentences are quoted and mocked in reviews  _(37)_
- Repeating a trait, phrase or explanation the reader already grasped reads as condescension and becomes a joke  _(33)_
- Itemized minutiae (meals, outfits, driving routes, weather, decor) is read as filler and skimmed  _(27)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- A hook on page one (a body, a shocking death, a killer first line, an abduction) is the opener readers cite and credit  _(51)_
- A strong start that collapses at the halfway mark is punished harder than a book that is weak throughout  _(43)_
- Front-loaded backstory or long character setup before the inciting crime loses critical readers by page 50-100  _(36)_
- A slow start is forgiven when a sinister undertone, strong voice or atmosphere is present from the first page  _(27)_
- Too many characters or narrators introduced at once leaves readers unable to tell who is who  _(15)_

**Middle**
- The middle sags at 40-65%: readers name the percentage where they started skimming or jumped to the end  _(87)_
- Dual timelines and alternating POVs that drip-feed secrets and converge are loved when each strand leaves you wanting the next  _(52)_
- Side plots and personal subplots must visibly feed the case; new strands sprouting mid-book read as clutter  _(32)_
- Repetitive loops (same search, same worry, suspect/clear, fake-out danger) read as padding and teach readers to stop believing  _(24)_
- POV shifts or a trial frame used to keep the reader artificially in the dark are seen through as withholding, not suspense  _(17)_

**Ending**
- Flat, rushed or 'where did that come from' endings are the most common reason a gripping read drops a star or two  _(71)_
- A culprit guessed early flattens the ending no matter how it is staged; reviewers name the exact page they knew  _(59)_
- A genuinely unforeseen twist that makes readers rewind the whole book is the strongest driver of five stars  _(50)_
- Readers want closure and the why explained; ambiguous or unanswered endings after a long mystery feel like a cheat  _(31)_
- Sticking the landing redeems a flawed book; several ratings were raised a star by the ending alone  _(23)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**The whodunit / twist reveal** `the_reveal`
- An unguessable reveal that makes everything before it click is the most rewarded scene; 'didn't see it coming' is top praise  _(84)_
- A reveal confirming what readers suspected early lands flat however it is staged; the wait becomes obligation  _(71)_
- A culprit chosen only to be unexpected, a twist from nowhere, or one contradicting character is called a cheat  _(31)_
- Clues must be there in hindsight; readers rewind to find the planted hints and praise fair play when they do  _(16)_
- The culprit must be a character properly met on the page, with motive and method explained  _(11)_

**The opening crime, body discovery and gore** `body_discovery`
- A brutal, brief crime or body in the first pages (posed corpse, bones at a dig) is the genre's handshake  _(27)_
- Graphic detail is accepted, even praised, when essential; repeated or shock-only gore caps the rating  _(24)_
- A gripping opening crime raises the bar; the fall-off afterward is punished harder  _(15)_
- A signature detail at the scene, or a copycat replicating an old crime, is quoted and remembered  _(7)_
- Decades-old remains or a cold case resurfacing alongside the present is a reliably loved setup  _(6)_

**Inside the killer's head, and the killer's taunts** `killer_pov`
- Chapters in the killer's or stalker's mind are relished when coherent, darkly funny and uncomfortably followable  _(26)_
- A killer who writes, texts or taunts the investigator, or a game with explicit rules, ratchets suspense  _(17)_
- A cunning villain readers half-root for, or a captor who believes he is loving, beats a cartoon or thin-motive one  _(15)_
- In series, 'the killer targets the detective personally' and crimes that exist only to taunt the hero feel worn  _(8)_
- A killer POV that stays surface-level or gimmicky, with no plot momentum, is 'just creepy', not a thriller  _(6)_

**Abduction, disappearance and the captive's return** `abduction_captivity`
- A child or partner taken early and fast is 'every parent's nightmare' and launches the book off to the races  _(19)_
- The parents' or victim's anguish, rendered moment to moment with grief honored, is what grips  _(12)_
- Captives who could flee but don't infuriate unless constraints and fear are made real; readers do the math  _(11)_
- Survivors who shrug off trauma to turn detective, or withhold a small secret for 90% of the book, break belief  _(8)_
- The aftermath of a return (family adjustment, what she is hiding) can grip as hard as the abduction  _(7)_

**Climax, showdown and rescue** `final_confrontation`
- A heart-racing showdown with cathartic justice against a loathsome villain is what readers remember  _(21)_
- Action lands only when readers have bought the hero and fear for who might die; otherwise it is noise  _(15)_
- Convenient rescues, the mandatory thunderstorm, a Hollywood finale or a tacked-on coda read as contrived  _(11)_
- A climax that never arrives, fizzles, or follows the predictable script (captured, partner arrives) is anticlimactic  _(10)_
- A muddled, hard-to-follow climax, or one after exhausting repetition, evaporates the charge built before it  _(9)_

**The investigation, suspect parade and red herrings** `investigation_and_suspects`
- Every suspect must look genuinely capable of the crime; the sketchy one the heroine trusts telegraphs the culprit  _(15)_
- The right dose of red herrings lets readers feel they have it sussed; bulk herrings that go on forever are padding  _(13)_
- Detectives should solve by detection against resisting witnesses; a culprit found by luck is unearned  _(13)_
- Procedural and forensic detail is enjoyed when woven into the pursuit; acronyms and lectures in bulk overwhelm  _(12)_
- Case files handed to civilians, ignored forensics or absent police break belief immediately  _(9)_

**Marriage secrets, abuse behind closed doors, no one believes her** `domestic_secrets`
- Control dynamics (money, isolation, bruises explained away) chill when recognizable; escalated to creative they lose belief  _(13)_
- Being a fly on the wall as a 'perfect' marriage unravels, with both partners lying, is a reliably addictive engine  _(12)_
- 'No one believes her' works when the reader also doubts; drunk blackouts as the unreliability device wear thin  _(10)_
- Romanticizing an abuser, framing abuse as desire, or rape as a plot device draws the angriest reviews  _(7)_
- Long stretches inside one paranoid, obsessive narrator's head exhaust; other POVs or flashbacks give relief  _(6)_

**The courtroom and the trial** `courtroom_trial`
- Courtroom chapters grip when procedure is right and the lawyer a convincing operator; lukewarm readers invest here  _(10)_
- Procedural howlers (unruled hearsay, impossible convictions) break trust more than any twist  _(4)_
- Readers cheer when the courtroom delivers justice the system otherwise couldn't  _(3)_
- A trial frame that hides who died or who is accused for half the book is withholding, not suspense  _(2)_

## Romance
<a id="romance"></a>

**Key:** `romance` · **Reviews:** 1,190 · **Matches genre labels like:** romance, romantic, love story, romantic comedy, rom-com, contemporary romance, historical romance, paranormal romance, regency, second chance, …

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Banter between the leads and laugh-out-loud humor are the most-cited pleasure; they buy forgiveness for a thin or familiar plot  _(168)_
- A hero with a hard shell and a shown devoted core (vulnerable, protective, treats her as an equal) carries the book even when the rest is weak  _(123)_
- A warm secondary cast (family, friends, prior-book couples) is a selling point; readers often name a side character as the best part  _(98)_
- Palpable chemistry and slow-built sexual tension are praised more often than the sex itself; 'worth the wait' is the ideal  _(95)_
- Readers want to be wrecked: tears, aching, 'I felt everything' is five-star language; emotional payoff outranks plot mechanics  _(81)_
- A heroine who holds her own, knows what she wants and stays believably flawed is called 'refreshing' and 'tragically rare'  _(79)_
- Unputdownable pacing earns forgiveness for almost everything else; 'read it in one sitting' is the top compliment  _(56)_
- Steam is expected and praised, but it almost never decides the rating on its own; heat lands when it carries emotion  _(42)_

**Readers punish**
- An immature, whiny, selfish or doormat heroine is the single most common reason readers rate low or quit; 'I wanted to shake her'  _(165)_
- Too long, too slow, nothing happens: readers name the pages to cut and skim; a couple alone is not a plot  _(105)_
- Insta-love or a rushed getting-to-know-you stage is not believed; readers must watch the connection form on the page  _(103)_
- A hero who is cruel, controlling or sleeps around under her nose is not redeemed by backstory; without consent and care it is abuse  _(102)_
- Predictable trope checklists and twists guessed in chapter one turn the rest of the book into an empty wait  _(91)_
- Leads with no chemistry, or a connection told rather than felt, kill the romance regardless of plot or prose  _(77)_
- Conflict one honest conversation would fix, and repeated break-up/make-up loops, read as manufactured drama  _(67)_
- Implausible logic, plot holes and detail the reader knows is wrong (geography, sport, accents) snap readers out  _(61)_

**They come in expecting**
- Readers pre-screen for cheating, love triangles and a guaranteed HEA; cheating by a lead is a near-universal dealbreaker  _(96)_
- An HEA (or clear HFN) is the contract; cliffhangers are accepted only when the series is signposted and a standalone must stand alone  _(89)_
- Readers know the tropes by name and judge execution, not novelty: a fresh twist on a worn trope is rewarded, played straight it bores  _(61)_
- Cover, blurb and label are a promise of heat level, subgenre and premise; a book that does not deliver them is a bait  _(50)_
- The love story must carry the book; suspense, sport, kitchen-sink subplots or sequel-couple setup that crowd it out are penalized  _(41)_

**On the prose itself**
- Repeated tics, pet names and catchphrases get literally tallied in reviews; one overused gesture can become the whole review  _(76)_
- Cheesy, stilted or speech-like dialogue is the most-quoted prose failing; 'who talks like that?' breaks immersion  _(70)_
- Lyrical, quotable, highlight-worthy lines earn praise and even a star when the plot disappoints  _(55)_
- One wrong word in a sex scene (petals, channel, engorged, back passage) kills the mood for the whole book  _(40)_
- Typos, lie/lay slips and POV errors are noticed and cost stars, especially in self-published books  _(34)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- Nothing to care about by 20-25% is the DNF point; a slow or confusing start is forgiven only when the book later pays off  _(49)_
- A brilliant first chapter the book cannot sustain is the signature disappointment; readers mourn the deflated five-star balloon  _(46)_
- Hooked from the first page or prologue predicts a five-star review and buys forgiveness for later flaws  _(35)_
- Lust or love in the first pages before character and setting exist earns an eye-roll or a DNF at 3%  _(13)_
- Most early abandonments cite a lead the reader cannot stand, not the plot  _(12)_

**Middle**
- The sagging middle is where skimming happens: lulls, rehashed conversations, repeated parties or standoffs  _(53)_
- Sex scene after sex scene with no feeling or plot between them is skimmed; readers want substance with their steam  _(38)_
- Break-up/make-up cycles and hot-and-cold push-pull work in doses; constant reversals exhaust by the midpoint  _(31)_
- Kitchen-sink subplots, sequel couples or a mid-book genre pivot dilute the romance readers signed up for  _(26)_
- A second half that turns around can rescue a wobbly first half, but '70% awful for 30% nice' is not worth it  _(22)_

**Ending**
- Endings are rated separately; an epilogue years into the future is the expected reward and can lift the whole book a star  _(81)_
- A rushed last 15% after a long build is the most common ending complaint; a climax 'explained in a paragraph' is unforgivable  _(51)_
- Cliffhangers thrill when the series is declared and feel like a scam when the book was sold as a standalone  _(40)_
- Forgiveness in five seconds, a neat bow or a grovel bought with a ring and flowers undercuts the HEA  _(35)_
- A predictable ending satisfies if the journey earned it; a late twist must be set up, not soap-opera shock  _(27)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**The first meeting** `first_meeting`
- An odd, specific or hilarious meet (pit trap, bear trap, mistaken identity, accidental email) is quoted and named the book's charm  _(50)_
- Stranger-turns-out-to-be-boss, or a one-night stand whose morning after drives the plot, is a beloved setup  _(30)_
- Love declared at the meet or within 48 hours is the most-mocked moment in the genre; sparks, not love  _(22)_
- A bickering or mutual-misjudgment first meeting is prized over a swooning one; it sets up the payoff  _(16)_
- The clumsy-heroine spilled-drink meet is recognized as done a thousand times; use it only with a twist  _(11)_

**The verbal sparring match** `banter_sparring`
- Witty combative back-and-forth is the most quoted scene type; readers paste exchanges into reviews  _(30)_
- Sparring reads as foreplay when both sides land hits and she can out-talk him or call his bluff  _(12)_
- Banter that curdles into cruelty, slut-shaming or crude insults makes readers say 'they deserve each other'  _(8)_
- A standoff that runs too long becomes long-winded; the ice must visibly crack  _(4)_

**The first sex scene** `first_intimacy`
- The first time must grow from built tension (rules, pacts, forbidden status, near-misses); one arriving 'out of nowhere' reads random  _(47)_
- The hottest scenes are praised for emotional intimacy and the couple's dynamic, not mechanics; rehearsed or inventory sex is skipped  _(34)_
- Readers police details: protection, plausible stamina, odd venues, and bedroom behaviour that matches each lead's established persona  _(25)_
- Steam is rated separately from the book; scorching sex can save a 3-star read and awkward sex can sink a 4-star one  _(20)_
- Heat must match the cover's promise; fade-to-black in a sexy pitch or explicit sex in a YA-feel book jars  _(16)_

**The secret comes out** `secret_reveal`
- A reveal that truly surprises and reframes the story is remembered as the book's high point  _(36)_
- A secret kept past 60-80% leaves readers no longer caring, and outright lying makes the liar unlovable  _(22)_
- When the reader guessed the secret chapters earlier, the long-delayed reveal lands flat  _(18)_
- After the reveal readers want proportionate reaction and fallout, not a page of gloss and instant forgiveness  _(14)_
- Soap-opera reveals (secret paternity, twins) or twists from nowhere draw head-shaking, not gasps  _(11)_

**The breakup / black moment** `black_moment`
- The black moment needs a cause talking could not fix; one unsaid sentence or noble self-sacrifice reads contrived  _(31)_
- Walking in on infidelity is the go-to rupture, but cheating by a lead is the one wound readers will not forgive  _(19)_
- A breakup that makes the heroine act out of character, or punish him for what he did not know, is hated more than the split  _(12)_
- Readers want the pain on the page, not summarized, and years of silence explained and answered for  _(12)_
- One black moment, not six; repeated break-up/make-up within a week exhausts  _(11)_

**The grovel, grand gesture and reunion** `grovel_reconciliation`
- Forgiveness granted in half a page is a cop-out; the grovel must be proportional to the harm and the hurt party's anger must last  _(27)_
- A second-chance reunion must carry the weight of lost time; reunion sex minutes after he walks back in breaks belief  _(25)_
- A single concrete, personal gesture lands harder than a speech; a limo, spa or two-carat ring rings false if nothing earned it  _(16)_
- A hero's patient tenacity in winning her back is swoon-worthy; pursuit by nagging or sabotaging her dates is a stalker  _(15)_
- Declaration lines are quoted as the heart of the book, and mocked when they go purple  _(14)_

**Danger, rescue and suspense** `danger_rescue`
- A suspense thread must be coherent, competent and solved, not wallpaper; serial kidnappings cheapen stakes  _(19)_
- When the action or mafia subplot overshadows the love story, readers say the romance got lost  _(17)_
- The hero stepping in to protect or rescue her, and losing control when she is in jeopardy, is described as hot  _(16)_
- Action set pieces keep readers going when the romance sags, and a cheesy rescue is enjoyed if the stakes were real  _(8)_
- Hero violence toward threats splits readers; violence toward the heroine is never romantic  _(5)_

**Family and friend-group scenes** `ensemble`
- Checking in with prior couples, siblings and townsfolk, and warm holiday-table scenes, are pleasures readers name explicitly  _(23)_
- When the ensemble takes the leads' page time, the main couple 'become secondary characters' in their own book  _(13)_
- Families that exist only as decor or 'mini minions' serving the leads are noticed  _(7)_

## Young adult
<a id="young-adult"></a>

**Key:** `young_adult` · **Reviews:** 1,190 · **Matches genre labels like:** young adult, ya, teen, coming-of-age, coming of age, high school, new adult, middle grade

### Generalised guidance

**Readers reward**
- Relentless forward motion and short, hooky chapters: 'read it in one sitting' is the highest praise and rescues flawed books  _(129)_
- Friendship, sibling, family and found-crew bonds given real weight, often above romance, are named as the heart of the book  _(114)_
- Flawed, relatable protagonists who make mistakes and visibly grow are the ones readers connect with and reread  _(90)_
- Rich, picturable world-building and magic readers 'want more of' is the single most memorable thing about a fantasy  _(76)_
- Humor that lands (snarky narration, banter, a comic sidekick) is quoted back and lifts even dark books  _(73)_
- A fully fleshed supporting cast with distinct voices can carry a book whose lead or plot disappoints  _(72)_
- A twist readers genuinely didn't see coming, yet that plays fair in hindsight, puts a book on favorites shelves  _(71)_
- Being emotionally wrecked (tears, 'the feels', ugly-crying) is the ultimate payoff and forgives much else  _(69)_

**Readers punish**
- Predictable, derivative plots and stacked cliches ('I've read this a hundred times') are called out trope by trope  _(133)_
- Flat, cardboard characters, especially the side cast, leave readers unable to care who lives or dies  _(129)_
- Stretches where 'nothing happens'; readers name the page count that should have been cut  _(124)_
- A whiny, bratty, self-pitying or recklessly stupid protagonist makes readers want to 'shake' her, whatever the plot  _(122)_
- Mental illness, suicide, abuse or assault used as plot device, quirk or romance fodder draws one-star fury  _(91)_
- Thin or hazy world-building: naming places is not building them; unexplained magic rules exhaust  _(75)_
- Insta-love is the most-named romance failure, often cited with the page number; its absence is praised  _(72)_
- Romance swamping the fantasy, sci-fi, mystery or serious plot is resented; 'no romance' is called a relief  _(68)_

**They come in expecting**
- Sequels must move the story, not rehash book one; a finale must resolve threads, not open new worlds  _(73)_
- Contrived coincidences, deus ex machina and implausible events break trust; contemporaries are held to realism  _(70)_
- Blurb, cover, comp titles and hype are a contract; a book that delivers a different premise is punished  _(69)_
- Love triangles are tolerated only when subtle or strategically sensible; obvious or padded ones are an eye-roll  _(64)_
- Retellings must keep the source's recognizable bones and heart while genuinely reinventing it  _(56)_

**On the prose itself**
- Lyrical, distinctive prose is quoted back in reviews and buys forgiveness for slow or confusing books  _(96)_
- Plain 'adequate' prose and repeated phrases, traits or inner rules are counted and quoted mockingly  _(88)_
- Dialogue and voice must sound like real teens; stilted, robotic, over-snarky or dumbed-down 'written for YA' voice jars  _(81)_
- Beautiful writing cannot save a thin plot or flat characters; readers note 'lovely prose' then DNF  _(31)_
- Purple prose and a metaphor in every paragraph read as trying too hard and distance the reader  _(27)_

### Where in the book

**Opening**
- Readers give roughly 100-150 pages; a plot direction and a likable lead must exist by then or they DNF  _(83)_
- Being hooked from the first line or first few pages is praise readers state explicitly and remember  _(38)_
- A strong premise that fizzles, or arrives 100+ pages late, angers readers more than a weak start  _(35)_
- Confusing first chapters (unexplained world, unclear POV, invented terms) lose readers before chapter five  _(20)_
- An unlikable lead at the start works only if visible change is promised early  _(9)_

**Middle**
- The repetitive middle (training loop, angst loop, parties, walking) is where readers skim or quit, usually 25-65%  _(86)_
- A strong last third is credited ('stars are for the second half') but many readers never reach it  _(19)_
- A mid-book turn that reframes the setup, or mini-cliffhangers between alternating POVs, keeps the middle alive  _(18)_
- Romance taking over mid-book and side characters vanishing for it is noticed and resented  _(16)_
- Action-light stretches are forgiven when constant danger, tension or voice carries them  _(14)_

**Ending**
- Rushed, abrupt or unresolved endings are the sharpest final-act complaint and can sink a whole series  _(88)_
- Earned closure for characters and threads, even with loss or heartbreak, is what earns five stars  _(63)_
- Cliffhangers after a real climax are adored; cut mid-scene in a disliked book they read as a marketing ploy  _(60)_
- A series finale must outdo the middle books and answer the questions raised; loose ends feel like betrayal  _(27)_
- Everything fixed by one conversation, or a tidy 'Hollywood' resolution with no catharsis, feels unearned  _(22)_

### Scene kinds (genre-specific sub-prompts)

**The big reveal or twist** `big_reveal`
- A reveal readers genuinely didn't see coming is rare in YA and celebrated as the book's best moment  _(25)_
- Seed clues readers can rewind to; a twist from nowhere is as punished as one called from page one  _(22)_
- A reveal telegraphed early, or a killer shown too soon, drains the rest of the book  _(15)_
- Answer the questions you raise; withholding known backstory past the midpoint reads as coyness  _(13)_
- A guessed twist is forgiven if the landing has emotional weight  _(10)_

**Character death and grief** `character_death`
- A death only devastates if the reader was made to love the character first; otherwise 'didn't bat an eye'  _(23)_
- Deaths for shock, to plug plot holes, or later undone by loophole are called cheap  _(14)_
- Grief rendered through daily texture, with hope and humor, earns the tears readers want  _(13)_
- A death readers didn't foresee that still feels inevitable is the most prized kind  _(6)_

**First kiss, confession and romantic payoff** `first_kiss`
- The kiss or confession lands after trust and tension are built; readers call it 'worth the wait'  _(33)_
- Banter and love-hate sparring before the romance is the most quoted material in the genre  _(33)_
- A kiss or 'I love you' within days, between people who showed no chemistry, is cited with the page number  _(26)_
- Heat level must match the voice; readers want more than chaste kisses and queer romance felt on the page  _(22)_
- Overwrought declarations ('a single frozen tear') draw laughs and get quoted back as ridicule  _(9)_

**Battle, fight and escape sequences** `battle_action`
- Action works only when danger could cost something; cartoon-safe peril is skimmed  _(21)_
- Clear, concrete staging that readers can visualize and that makes tactical sense earns praise  _(14)_
- A heroine picking up the sword or using her own powers, instead of agonizing, is a cheered moment  _(13)_
- A promised war or climax that happens offstage or fizzles is the biggest letdown  _(11)_
- Readers count the action scenes and want them early and recurring, not saved for the end  _(10)_

**Training, trials and competitions** `training_trials`
- A competition or trial structure gives the middle a spine when stakes escalate every round  _(14)_
- Training drags unless each session reveals character or carries stakes; long prep gets skipped  _(9)_
- Learning the rules (poisons, powers, spycraft) step by step with a mentor is a warm, returned-to spot  _(9)_
- Put the stages on the page with real risk; skipping to results or risk-free trials bores  _(8)_

**School cliques, bullying and friend-group drama** `school_cruelty`
- Petty friend-group and mean-girl drama exhausts goodwill fast; 'good kids' are a relief  _(33)_
- Bullying bites when specific and sensory, implicates bystanders, and deals with the hurt afterward  _(16)_
- School life (classes, dorms, detention) works when it generates plot, not hallway wallpaper  _(13)_
- 'New girl, new town, mysterious boy' is recognized as formula instantly and needs a twist  _(10)_

**Parents, siblings and kitchen-table scenes** `family_scene`
- Present, flawed-but-loving parents and fully drawn siblings are rare enough that readers celebrate them  _(20)_
- Absent, useless or stereotyped adults who don't react break belief  _(13)_
- A family blow-up resolved in one conversation cannot fix years of hurt and feels unearned  _(6)_
- Family dropped the moment the romance starts angers readers  _(3)_

**Breakdown, trauma and recovery scenes** `mental_health_crisis`
- Readers with lived experience judge these scenes; get the specific symptoms and research right  _(16)_
- A crisis 'fixed' by a love interest or an external trick is the most despised move  _(15)_
- Raw, unsugar-coated struggle that spends more time on recovery than on the hurt is called important  _(13)_
- Content warnings are expected; reviewers supply them when the book doesn't  _(7)_
- Therapy and asking for help should be modeled, not a friend's DIY cure  _(7)_
